Ask three studios what your app will cost to design and you will get three numbers that look like they belong to different industries. Five hundred dollars. Eight thousand. Forty thousand. All three quotes can be honest. All three can describe completely different work. That is the whole problem with UI/UX design pricing: the word "design" is doing far too much lifting, and until you pin down what sits underneath it, every quote is a guess wearing a suit.

This guide is the one we wish clients read before they email us. It is written from the quoting side of the table — what we actually charge at AISquadX, what the numbers mean, where the traps are, and how to buy design work without overpaying or underbuying. Every range below is our observed market range for 2026, in USD and PKR, drawn from quoting and delivering this work for clients in the US, the UK and Pakistan.

## Why one quote says $800 and another says $40,000

They are not quoting the same thing. They never are. When design quotes diverge wildly, five variables are almost always responsible — and only one of them is the hourly rate.

**1. Scope definition.** A quote for "design our app" might mean fifteen screens or eighty. It might include onboarding, settings, empty states and error screens — or none of those, which is where junior quotes quietly save money and where products quietly fall apart later. The unglamorous screens (empty states, error handling, permissions flows) are typically 30–40% of a real product's interface. Quotes that skip them are cheaper and incomplete.

**2. Research depth.** Some quotes include user interviews, analytics review, competitor teardown and usability testing. Others include none of that and start at "we'll make it look good." Research is the most expensive line item to include and the most expensive one to skip. We have watched $25,000 builds get thrown away because nobody spent the $3,000 to check what users actually needed first.

**3. Iteration rounds.** Two revision rounds versus unlimited revisions is a different project. Unlimited revisions sound generous; in practice they mean the scope was never agreed and the schedule is fiction. Serious studios cap revisions (usually two to three structured rounds) because constraints produce decisions.

**4. Who does the work.** A senior designer with eight years of SaaS experience and a junior on their first commercial project both sell "UI design." The senior finishes in half the time, asks the questions that prevent rework, and designs for the edge cases. The rate gap — often 3–5x — is mostly an experience gap, and it usually pays for itself in avoided rebuilds.

**5. What is excluded.** Copywriting, custom illustration, photography direction, icon sets, motion design, accessibility remediation, developer handoff support. Each of these is a real cost centre. A quote that excludes all of them looks cheap; the client pays for them later, usually at rush rates.

None of this means expensive is good and cheap is bad. It means the number is meaningless until you know what it covers. The rest of this guide makes the coverage legible.

## The four ways studios actually price design work

Strip away the branding and there are four pricing models in the market. Each has a natural habitat and a characteristic failure mode.

**Hourly billing.** The studio charges per hour worked, tracked and reported. Typical 2026 ranges: $25–$80/hr for freelancers and small studios in Pakistan, $50–$150/hr for established Pakistani studios, $150–$350/hr for US and UK studios. Independent 2026 breakdowns show the same pattern: [Dribbble's agency-cost guide](https://dribbble.com/resources/tips/ui-ux-design-agency-costs) puts US senior designers at $100–$250/hr versus $50–$150/hr in South Asia, and [Orbix Studio's September 2026 guide](https://www.orbix.studio/blogs/ux-ui-design-cost-guide) prices agency hourly rates at $100–$300/hr. Hourly is honest for exploratory work — early product definition, ongoing iteration, design support during development — where nobody can scope the end in advance. The failure mode is drift: without a weekly cap or a burn report, hours accumulate and the client discovers the total at the invoice. If you buy hourly, insist on a weekly hour cap and a one-page weekly log. Any studio that resists that is telling you something.

**Fixed project price.** One number for a defined deliverable list. This is the right model for anything with a knowable scope: landing pages, marketing sites, a defined set of app screens. In our experience, fixed pricing produces the best design because it forces both sides to agree on scope before anyone opens Figma. That matches startup-focused 2026 data: [MDX's startup pricing guide](https://mdx.so/blog/ui-ux-design-cost-guide-startups?id=811) puts a mid-scope product (15–25 screens, some research, one round of testing) at $15,000–$30,000, and a full product design with a design system at $35,000–$60,000. The failure mode is the vague fixed bid — "redesign our website, $5,000" — where scope was never defined and every screen becomes a negotiation. A fixed quote without a deliverable list is not a quote; it is an opening bid for an argument.

**Monthly retainer.** A flat monthly fee for a set amount of design capacity — typically 40–160 hours a month. Retainers make sense for startups iterating fast or companies with continuous design needs (new features, landing pages, experiments). Pakistan-based studio retainers run $1,500–$6,000/month; US equivalents run $8,000–$25,000/month. The failure mode is the unused retainer: you pay for capacity you do not use. Retainers work when you have a real backlog; they are a donation when you do not.

**Value or performance pricing.** Rare in pure design, more common in conversion-focused redesigns: a base fee plus a bonus tied to a metric (conversion rate, signup growth). We like the principle and almost never see it done well, because isolating design's contribution from marketing, pricing and seasonality is genuinely hard. Treat it as a bonus structure on top of a fair base, never as the whole deal.

| Model | Typical range (PKT / US) | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hourly | $25–$150/hr PK / $150–$350/hr US | Exploratory work, ongoing iteration | Hour drift without caps |
| Fixed project | $500–$60,000+ per project | Defined scope: sites, landing pages, app v1 | Vague scope disguised as a fixed number |
| Monthly retainer | $1,500–$6,000/mo PK / $8,000–$25,000/mo US | Continuous design needs, fast startups | Paying for unused capacity |
| Value-based | Base + performance bonus | Conversion redesigns with clean metrics | Attribution fights over what caused the lift |

Our position, stated plainly: fixed price for defined work, hourly or retainer for undefined work, and never a fixed price with an undefined scope. That one rule would eliminate most design-client disputes we have ever seen.

## What you are actually buying: the deliverable ladder

Not all "design" includes the same artefacts. Here is the ladder, from cheapest to most complete, with what each rung typically costs from a mid-tier studio.

**Rung 1 — Screens only.** High-fidelity mockups of the agreed screens, delivered as Figma files. No research, no wireframes, no testing. This is what most $500–$2,000 quotes are selling. It is fine when you know exactly what you need and just need it drawn well — a landing page refresh, a pitch deck site. It is not product design; it is illustration with buttons.

**Rung 2 — UX + UI.** Wireframes and user flows first, then high-fidelity screens, then an interactive prototype. The designer thinks through the structure before polishing the surface. This is the minimum for anything users log into. Typical range: $3,000–$15,000 depending on screen count.

**Rung 3 — UX + UI + research and testing.** Everything in rung 2, plus user interviews or surveys, usability testing of the prototype with real users, and iteration based on findings. This is where design stops being decoration and starts being evidence. Typical range: $8,000–$30,000. The testing round alone usually catches two or three issues that would have cost ten times more to fix after development.

**Rung 4 — Full product design with a design system.** Everything in rung 3, plus a documented component library (buttons, inputs, cards, modals, states), design tokens for colour and spacing, accessibility specs, and a structured developer handoff. This is what serious SaaS products and funded startups buy. Typical range: $15,000–$60,000+.


The rung matters more than the rate. A $150/hr designer selling rung 1 delivers less value than a $60/hr designer selling rung 3, if what you needed was rung 3. When a quote looks surprisingly cheap, check which rung it is actually on before comparing numbers.

One more thing buyers miss: the handoff. A design that developers cannot build from is not finished, whatever the invoice says. Proper handoff — annotated specs, exportable assets, responsive behaviour defined, empty and error states covered — is part of the work, not a favour. Ask every studio what their handoff includes. The answer tells you whether they have shipped real products or just portfolios.

Three honest observations from working across these markets.

First, the Pakistan discount is a labour-market fact, not a quality verdict. Senior designers in Lahore and Karachi produce work indistinguishable from their London counterparts — many trained at the same online schools, use the same tools, and freelance for the same Western clients. The rate difference reflects cost of living and local demand, not capability. ## The hidden costs that blow design budgets

Most budget overruns do not come from the design fee. They come from the things around it that nobody quoted. Startup-focused 2026 research [puts those unquoted extras at 20–35% on top of the quoted price](https://mdx.so/blog/ui-ux-design-cost-guide-startups?id=811). Here is the full list, with what each typically adds.

**Copywriting.** Designers are not copywriters, and lorem ipsum is not a content strategy. Real copy for a 10-page marketing site runs $1,000–$4,000 from a decent writer. Clients who plan to "write it themselves" are the number one cause of design delays we see — the design waits on copy, the copy waits on the founder, the schedule quietly dies. Budget the copy or accept the delay; do not pretend neither exists.

**Custom illustration and photography.** Stock imagery is cheap and looks it. A custom illustration set for a product runs $1,500–$6,000; a half-day product photography shoot, $800–$3,000. Decide early whether the brand needs bespoke visuals, because bolting them on after the design is finished means redesigning around them.

**Motion and micro-interaction.** Page transitions, animated onboarding, Lottie animations, scroll choreography. Adds $1,000–$5,000 to a typical project and disproportionate perceived quality — motion is the cheapest way to make a product feel expensive. But it must be designed, not improvised by the developer, or you get jank.

**Accessibility remediation.** Designing to WCAG contrast and touch-target standards from the start costs almost nothing extra. Retrofitting accessibility onto a finished design costs 20–40% of the original fee. If your market includes the US, UK or EU, accessibility is not optional — it is legal exposure. Specify it in the brief.

**Developer handoff friction.** This is the silent budget killer. A design delivered as flat mockups with no specs, no responsive rules and no asset exports forces developers to make design decisions at $100+/hr — badly, because that is not their job. Proper handoff (component specs, redlines, exported assets, behaviour notes) is part of a professional engagement. If a quote does not mention handoff, assume you are buying rung 1 and budgeting rung 3's development anyway.

**Revision loops.** Every undefined revision round costs roughly 10–15% of the project fee in designer time and schedule. The fix is contractual, not interpersonal: define what a "round" means (consolidated feedback, one decision-maker, 5 business days), cap the rounds, and price additional rounds upfront. Studios that do this are not being difficult; they are protecting your timeline.

**Stakeholder count.** The most underpriced variable in design. Each additional approver with veto power adds roughly one revision cycle and two weeks. A project with one decisive product owner and a project with a committee of six are different projects at the same screen count. If you are the client, nominate one decision-maker. It is the highest-ROI move in this entire guide.
## Red flags in a design quote (and the green ones)

After years of quoting against competitors, we have a reliable checklist. Five red flags that predict a bad engagement:

**1. No deliverable list.** "Complete UX/UI design: $4,000." Complete means nothing. A real quote lists artefacts: research plan, wireframe count or scope, screen list, prototype, testing rounds, handoff package. No list, no deal.

**2. Unlimited revisions.** Sounds client-friendly; signals that scope was never defined. Professional studios limit revisions because they have learned — expensively — that unlimited means unfinishable.

**3. No research or testing line item.** If the quote jumps straight to high-fidelity screens, you are buying decoration. For a marketing site that may be fine. For a product, it is malpractice with good kerning.

**4. Portfolio only, no process.** Beautiful Dribbble shots tell you someone can make attractive rectangles. Ask how they handle developer handoff, what their revision policy is, and how they test with users. Vague answers here predict a vague project.

**5. The price is dramatically below market with no explanation.** Sometimes it is a hungry junior building a portfolio — legitimate, if you know what you are getting. More often it means the scope is hollow: screens without states, no mobile, no handoff, and change orders for everything else. Ask what is excluded. The answer is the real quote.

And three green flags: the studio asks about your users and business goals before talking about screens; the quote names what is *not* included; and they show you a Figma file structure or handoff example from a past project. That last one is the single best predictor we know — a studio proud of its handoff has shipped real products.


## How to buy UX design without overpaying

You do not need the most expensive studio. You need the right scope, bought the right way. Here is the process we recommend to every prospective client, including the ones who end up hiring someone else.

**Step 1: Write a one-page brief before you talk to anyone.** What the product does, who it is for, the three most important user tasks, your timeline, your budget range, and who decides. This single page will improve the quotes you receive more than any negotiation tactic. Studios quote vaguely when the brief is vague — they have to pad for the unknown.

**Step 2: Decide which rung you need.** Marketing site refresh? Rung 1 or 2 is fine. New SaaS product? Rung 3 minimum; rung 4 if you plan to scale the team. Internal dashboard used by twelve employees? Rung 2 — usability matters, polish less so. Buying rung 4 for a landing page is overpaying; buying rung 1 for a fintech app is underbuying, and the rebuild will cost more than the saving.

**Step 3: Get three quotes and compare coverage, not numbers.** Line up the deliverable lists side by side. The cheapest quote that covers your rung is usually the right answer. A quote 50% below the others is not a bargain; it is a different rung wearing your rung's label.

**Step 4: Freelancer, studio, or in-house?** A freelancer is right for small, well-defined work and for augmenting a team. A studio is right for end-to-end product design, when you need research plus UI plus handoff without hiring three people. In-house is right when design is continuous and core to the product — roughly, when you would keep a designer busy for a year. The common mistake is hiring a freelancer for a studio-sized job, then paying a studio to fix it. We see this monthly.

**Step 5: Start with an audit or a paid trial.** A [UX audit](/services) of your existing product ($1,500–$5,000) or a small paid pilot (one flow, fixed price) tells you everything about how a studio works — communication, rigour, handoff quality — before you commit to the full engagement. It is the cheapest insurance in the buying process.

One more piece of judgement, offered freely: the design market in 2026 is splitting. AI tools have made rung-1 work (attractive screens, fast) dramatically cheaper to produce — and therefore a worse differentiator. What AI cannot do is the rung-3 work: talking to your actual users, making trade-off calls about complex workflows, and owning the handoff into a real codebase. That is where the value — and the pricing power — is moving. Buy accordingly. ## Frequently asked questions

**How much does UI/UX design cost in 2026?**
A landing page refresh starts around $500–$1,500, a full marketing website runs $3,000–$12,000, and a complete web or mobile app product design with research, testing and a component library typically costs $8,000–$60,000+. In Pakistan, studio rates run 40–60% below US rates for comparable work. The number moves with scope, research depth, revision rounds and who is doing the work.

**Do freelancers or agencies charge more for UX design?**
Freelancers usually charge less per hour — $25–$80 in Pakistan, $75–$200 in the US — while studios charge $50–$150/hr in Pakistan and $150–$350/hr in the US. But the comparison is rarely apples to apples: a freelancer sells one person's time, a studio sells a team (researcher, designer, motion, handoff support) and process. For anything bigger than a landing page, the studio quote often covers work a freelancer would bill as extras.

**What is included in a UI/UX design project?**
A proper engagement includes discovery and user research, information architecture, wireframes, high-fidelity UI screens, a component library or design system, an interactive prototype, usability testing with real users, and a developer handoff package with specs and assets. Cheap quotes usually strip this down to screens only — no research, no testing, no system — which is exactly where redesigns go wrong.

**How much does a UX audit cost?**
A focused UX audit of an existing website or app costs $1,500–$5,000 at most studios (PKR 400,000–1,400,000), covering heuristic evaluation, analytics and session review, and a prioritised findings report. Audits with moderated user testing run higher. It is the cheapest design money you can spend: it tells you whether you need a full redesign or just targeted fixes.

**Why do design quotes vary so much?**
Because "design" means different scopes to different sellers. One quote covers ten high-fidelity screens; another covers research, testing, a design system and three revision rounds. Quotes also diverge on who does the work (junior freelancer vs senior team), what is excluded (copywriting, illustration, motion), and how revisions are handled. The spread is usually a scope difference, not a pricing conspiracy.

**Should I pay hourly or fixed price for design work?**
Fixed price for well-defined projects (landing page, marketing site, defined app scope) — it forces the studio to scope carefully. Hourly or weekly for exploratory work (early-stage products, ongoing iteration) where the scope genuinely cannot be fixed upfront. Avoid the worst of both worlds: a fixed price with vague scope, which guarantees a fight about what counts as a revision.
