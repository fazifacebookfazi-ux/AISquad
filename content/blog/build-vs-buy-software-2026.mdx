## The Renewal Email Nobody Budgeted For

Your SaaS renewal lands. The number is bigger than last year. Not a little bigger — a fifth, a third bigger. You open the line items and there it is: an AI tier you never asked for, bundled into the base plan, charged per seat for features half your team has never opened.

This is happening everywhere right now, and it has quietly rewritten the oldest question in software: do we build it, or do we buy it?

For a decade, "buy" was the default answer, and it was usually right. SaaS was cheap, per-seat pricing was predictable, and building your own tools looked like vanity engineering. But the ground shifted under that logic this year. Vendors across CRM, help desk, analytics, and design software ran the same playbook through 2026: fold the AI in, retire the cheaper SKU, and let the renewal do the persuading. Microsoft's July 2026 packaging update raised commercial Microsoft 365 list prices 5 to 43 percent across Business, Enterprise, Frontline, and Government suites, with Copilot Chat now folded into the base of most tiers. You're paying for the AI capacity whether or not anyone on your team uses it.

At the same time, something deeper is eroding the per-seat model itself. AI agents and automation are doing work that used to take several seats of human clicking, so corporate clients are cutting seat counts. The industry calls it the seat compression effect, and [recent analysis](https://www.jpost.com/business-and-innovation/article-908502) shows margins at traditional SaaS vendors taking a real hit from it. Gartner's current forecast: by the end of 2026, over 80 percent of software providers incorporating AI will be forced to abandon traditional per-seat pricing for consumption- or outcome-based models. McKinsey's parallel finding is blunter — companies that fail to adapt their financial infrastructure to these dynamic models suffer double-digit erosion in gross margins.

So the question isn't whether the math changed. It's whether anyone at your company has rerun the numbers since it did. This article is the studio's full framework for doing exactly that — built from what we see building custom tools for founders every week.

## Why 2026 Broke the Old Answer

Let's be precise about what changed, because the build-vs-buy calculus has always been a math problem, and the inputs are different now.

**Input one: your subscription costs are no longer predictable.** SaaS management firm Zylo's 2026 SaaS Management Index, built from over 40 million licences and $75 billion in spend under management, found AI application spending jumping nearly 400 percent year over year inside companies with 10,000-plus employees — while total SaaS portfolios looked "stable at a glance." [CFO Brew's coverage](https://www.cfobrew.com/stories/2026/02/17/saas-budgets-bulge-due-to-ai) puts it well: AI is becoming the most expensive invisible worker in the organisation, adding opaque, usage-driven expenses that are harder to forecast and govern. When you can't forecast a cost line, you can't honestly compare it to anything.

**Input two: the per-seat meter is leaking.** Usage-based and outcome-based pricing is replacing seats. Ema, an enterprise AI startup, [raised a $77 million Series B in September](https://techstartups.com/2026/09/23/venture-capital-startup-funding-roundup-september-23-2026-accel-jpmorgan-s32-t-rowe-price-y-combinator-more/) explicitly around outcome-based pricing — paid for completed processes rather than seats — reporting multiyear contract bookings above $150 million and net dollar retention near 180 percent. The founders get the model. The question is whether the old per-seat tools in your stack have anywhere to go but up.

**Input three: building got cheaper while buying got weirder.** AI-assisted development hasn't replaced engineers, but it has compressed build timelines for standard business tools dramatically. What took a four-person team five months in 2023 takes two people ten weeks today. Meanwhile buying now means negotiating AI line items, credit bundles, and consumption meters. The comparison used to be "predictable subscription vs expensive bespoke project." Now it's "unpredictable subscription vs increasingly standardised build." That's a different fight.

None of this means buying is wrong. It means the answer has to be recomputed, and most companies haven't.
## The Only Comparison That Matters: Three Years, Everything Included

The mistake almost everyone makes is comparing the wrong numbers. They compare this year's subscription invoice against the quote from a development studio. That framing is rigged — of course the subscription is smaller. You're comparing rent to buying a house and stopping at the deposit.

The honest comparison is total cost of ownership across a three-year window, with every hidden line item dragged into the light.

On the **buy** side:

- Annual subscription fees × 3, with 10–20 percent assumed yearly increases (SaaS prices have risen consistently; vendors rarely freeze pricing for three years straight).
- Per-seat AI add-ons and credit bundles — the line item that barely existed in 2024 and is now the fastest-growing one.
- The internal hours spent keeping the stack talking to itself: integrations, field mapping, identity syncing, babysitting connectors when a vendor pushes a breaking API change. Value that time honestly. This is the biggest number founders pretend doesn't exist.
- Data extraction costs: the ETL or reverse-ETL spend to get your own data out of vendors that would rather keep it in.

On the **build** side:

- Scoped development cost (we'll give real numbers below).
- Hosting × 36 months.
- A maintenance retainer — plan for one, even if it's light.

Here's what that looks like for a realistic mid-sized workflow: a 25-person company with eight SaaS tools covering CRM, support, analytics, project tracking, and internal ops.

Capital is flowing toward AI-enabled software but paying much lower multiples for plain SaaS businesses. The market is repricing "software company" versus "commodity SaaS with a logo." Your renewal invoices are the customer-side version of the same repricing.

## The Four Tests: Our Studio's Framework

Numbers alone don't settle this — a cheap build of the wrong thing is still a waste. When founders ask us "should we build this?", we run four tests. This is adapted from the [cleanest public framework on the subject](https://www.streetregister.com/2026/09/21/the-ai-surcharge-on-your-saas-renewal-is-rewriting-the-build/), hardened by what we've seen go wrong in practice.

### Test 1: Is the workflow core or commodity?

Commodity layers belong on somebody else's roadmap: email, calendar, accounting, payroll, HR, file storage, video calls. These vendors have decades of security hardening, compliance certifications, and edge-case handling baked in. Rebuilding them is not differentiation — it's a hobby.

A build earns its keep when the workflow is one of the things that differentiates your business. Your client onboarding sequence. Your quoting and estimation process. The approval chain that holds your margins together. The proprietary way you score leads, triage support tickets, or schedule crews. If the SaaS product forces you to bend a winning process to fit its generic model, that's a signal. If your team keeps saying "we can't do it that way because the tool doesn't allow it" about something that makes you money — pay attention.

Our judgment call, and we take a position here: **most companies build too little of their differentiating workflows and too much commodity plumbing.** The typical failed build we get called in to rescue is an accounting system someone wrote from scratch. The typical failed SaaS stack we untangle is five generic tools that can't express the company's actual process.

### Test 2: Does the stack have more connectors than users?

Count the integration points. If you're running eight tools and maintaining dozens of fragile connections between them — Zaps, webhooks, middleware, nightly CSV dumps that break every time a vendor ships an update — you're not running software, you're running a museum of workarounds.

The math gets ugly fast. A stack of eight tools doesn't have eight relationships to maintain; it has dozens of possible pairs, and the pairs you actually connect are the ones most likely to break. Every new tool arrives with a second, invisible invoice: somebody has to wire it into the CRM, sync the identity provider, map the fields, and babysit the connector forever. That work never lands on a purchase order. It shows up as the hours your operations lead isn't spending on customers.

A custom tool that consolidates two or three of those jobs into one system, sitting on top of your own database, doesn't add a connector — it removes them. If integration and cleanup labour is approaching the cost of the tools themselves, and the fragility shows up in your support queue, the stack is telling you something.

### Test 3: Has the renewal math flipped?

Run the three-year comparison from the previous section on the actual workflow, not the whole company. If the buy column is bigger — with the AI surcharges, the price-increase assumptions, and the admin labour included — then continuing to rent is the expensive choice, not the safe one.

One nuance: weight recent price behaviour, not list prices. Vendors rarely cut prices. The Microsoft July 2026 move — 5 to 43 percent increases with Copilot folded in — is the template everyone else is copying. Your renewal next year will not be lower than this year's. Model 10–20 percent annual growth in the buy column and see if the answer changes.

### Test 4: Is your data stuck?

Can you answer a real business question — "which customer segment actually made us money last quarter?" — without exporting from four tools and stitching spreadsheets? If the information you need lives across five vendors and none of them want to give it back cleanly, you have a data hostage situation. Building on your own database is the only durable fix. Dashboards built on rented data rot at the speed of the vendor's roadmap.

**If two or more of these tests point to build, the renewal is the wrong instrument and the build conversation is overdue.** If none apply, renew the tool and negotiate hard on the AI line item.

## When You Do Buy: The Renewal Negotiation Playbook

Sometimes the worksheet says "renew." That's a legitimate outcome, and when it does, you owe it to yourself to negotiate like the vendor needs you more than you need them — because with seat counts compressing, they do.

**Benchmark the AI line first.** Before the renewal call, check what competitors charge for equivalent AI features and ask for the AI tier to be unbundled or discounted for seats that never use it. Vendors in 2026 are much more flexible on AI add-ons than on base licences, because the AI line is where they're experimenting on pricing.

**Use the build quote as leverage.** Get a fixed-scope build quote for the workflow (even if you never intend to build). Walking into a renewal with "your competitor's tool is $X and a custom build is $Y over three years" changes the dynamic instantly. You're no longer a captive renewer; you're a buyer with an exit option.

**Push for a multi-year lock without the AI escalation clause.** If the vendor wants a three-year commitment, the price should be fixed — including the AI tier. Refuse contracts where the base is locked but the "AI services" line floats. That's where the margin extraction hides.

**Audit usage before you renew.** Zylo's data suggests most companies have meaningful shadow AI spend — employees expensing tools outside the official stack. Consolidate that shadow spend into your negotiated contract and you often fund the renewal increase without new budget. And drop the seats nobody uses; seat compression should cut your bill, not grow it.

Buying well is a skill. Building well is a bigger one. Know which one each workflow deserves.

## The Honest List of What Goes Wrong When You Build

We sell builds for a living. We'll still tell you the failure modes, because the founders who ask about them make better decisions.

**Scope creep eats budgets.** "While you're in there, could you also…" is the most expensive sentence in software. The fix: a written scope with a phase-two list. Everything new goes to phase two. This isn't stinginess — it's how builds ship on time.

**Nobody maintains it.** The build finishes, the studio hands over the keys, and the company assigns maintenance to someone who already has a full-time job. Six months later there's a dependency update backlog and a small panic. The fix: budget the retainer from day one. It's 15–25 percent of the build cost per year, and it turns a project into an asset.

**You hire the cheapest bidder.** A $12,000 build that never ships costs more than a $40,000 build that does. Judge studios on shipped work and post-launch behaviour, not proposals. Ask for references from clients two years after launch, not two weeks.

**AI features without cost modelling.** This is the 2026-specific trap. An AI feature priced per token with no per-user cost model is a grenade with the pin halfway out. Model the cost per active user, set hard usage caps, and pass costs through in your own pricing if the tool touches customers. Our [SaaS pricing strategy guide](/blog/saas-pricing-strategy-2026) covers how to think about pricing AI value without drowning in your own meter.

**You build a commodity anyway.** The number-one cause of build regret we see: a company rebuilds something Zendesk, QuickBooks, or Google Workspace already does well, then discovers why those vendors charge what they charge — thousands of edge cases, security audits, and compliance paperwork. Test 1 exists to stop this. Trust it.

## What You Should Never Build, Full Stop

A short list, because this deserves bluntness:

- **Email, calendar, office suite.** Microsoft and Google have this handled. Don't be a hero.
- **Accounting and payroll.** Regulatory surface area you do not want to own.
- **HR systems and applicant tracking.** Same. Compliance nightmares, commodity features.
- **Core authentication infrastructure.** Use established providers. Rolling your own auth in 2026 is not engineering — it's liability.
- **Video conferencing, file storage, chat.** Solved problems. Rent them.
- **Anything with payment compliance.** Use Stripe or equivalent. Never store card data yourself unless that is literally your regulated business.

The principle: build the things that make you different; rent the things that make you normal. Being normal is not the enemy — it's the baseline that frees budget for the parts that matter.

## FAQ

**Is it cheaper to build or buy software in 2026?**

Buying wins on day one; building usually wins on three-year total cost for workflows that matter. The crossover moved earlier this year because AI surcharges inflated renewals while AI-assisted development compressed build timelines. Run the numbers per workflow, not per company.

**What is the build vs buy decision?**

It's the choice between licensing off-the-shelf software and commissioning a custom build. **How do you calculate the crossover point?**
