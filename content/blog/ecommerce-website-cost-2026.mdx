All three of them, probably. That is the infuriating thing about ecommerce pricing: "a website" is not a thing you buy, like a laptop. It is a thing you scope, like a building. A food stall and a restaurant both sell meals, and nobody is confused when they cost different amounts. Online stores deserve the same honesty.

The GoodFirms 2026 numbers deserve a second look because they tell you where the market sits: 63% of agencies quoted fixed-price web projects between $1,000 and $15,000, and custom web application builds cluster at $20,000–$50,000. So when someone quotes you $100,000 for a standard store, you are not buying a website — you are buying someone's overhead. And when someone promises a custom build for $3,000, you are buying a template with a new logo.

Clutch's 2026 buyer survey, cited in agency pricing guides updated this month, adds a shift worth knowing: 61% of small business buyers spent under $10,000 on their most recent website project — down from 68% a few years back. Buyers are spending less on the average project but demanding more from it: custom design and conversion-focused features, not brochure sites. The budget moved toward quality at the top end, and toward DIY at the bottom. The middle — a competent platform store, well executed — is where the smart money lands.

### The question that sets your price

Before any agency can quote you honestly, they need one number you probably haven't decided: how much complexity is in the buying process itself.

- **Simple:** under 100 products, one currency, one shipping setup, one payment gateway. This is a store, full stop.
- **Moderate:** 100–1,000 products with variants, a couple of gateways, discount rules, basic integrations (email marketing, accounting).
- **Complex:** multi-currency and multi-language, subscriptions, custom product configurators, B2B pricing tiers, ERP or warehouse integrations.

A "simple" store on Shopify can be excellent and cost $8,000. A "complex" store on a custom build can cost $120,000 and still be money well spent. Almost every pricing argument on the internet is two people describing different complexity tiers without saying so. When you get a quote, ask which tier the agency assumed. That single question will reveal more than any itemised proposal.

## The five ways people actually build stores in 2026 — and what each costs

Forget the platform marketing. Here is how each route behaves in practice, with the costs the brochures leave out.

![Abstract illustration of two diverging paths — a polished storefront on one side, a custom gear-like structure on the other — representing the platform vs custom build decision](/blog/ecommerce-website-cost-2026/fig1.webp)

### 1. Shopify: the default, and usually the right one

Shopify is the Toyota Corolla of ecommerce: boring, reliable, and exactly what most people need. Plans run $29–$299 per month on annual billing ($39–$399 month-to-month), per [Shopify's own 2026 development guide](https://www.shopify.com/my/blog/ecommerce-website-development). A DIY setup with a free theme and one gateway costs you mostly time; roughly $800 covers a year of Shopify Basic on annual billing plus a domain and a few paid apps, according to that same guide.

Where the money goes with an agency: theme customisation ($2,000–$8,000), custom sections and checkout branding ($1,500–$5,000), product data entry and photography direction, app configuration, and conversion setup (abandoned cart flows, reviews, upsells). A professional Shopify build lands at $5,000–$20,000 in the US, PKR 600,000–2,500,000 in Pakistan.

The honest downsides: transaction fees if you don't use Shopify Payments (0.5–2% on top of the gateway's cut), app subscription creep, and limited checkout customisation below the Plus tier. In countries where Shopify Payments is unavailable — Pakistan included — you will route through 2Checkout, Payoneer-linked gateways or local options, and international card acceptance needs testing, not assumptions. We have seen Pakistani exporters lose weeks discovering their "working" checkout rejected foreign cards. Test with real transactions from real countries before launch. Every time.

### 2. WooCommerce: ownership with a maintenance bill

WooCommerce is free. That sentence has cost more founders money than any other in ecommerce. The plugin costs nothing; the build around it doesn't. You need hosting that can handle traffic spikes ($30–$300/month for decent managed WooCommerce hosting), a premium theme or custom design, and paid extensions — subscriptions, bookings, multi-currency, and advanced shipping each run $79–$249 per year, and a real store accumulates five to ten of them. Developer rates run $25–$49 per hour per Clutch data cited in 2026 pricing guides.

A professionally built WooCommerce store lands at $8,000–$35,000 in the US, PKR 1,000,000–4,000,000 in Pakistan. Why would anyone pay more than Shopify for it? Two reasons that are actually valid: you keep full ownership with no monthly platform fee, and there is no scenario where a platform policy change affects your business. For US businesses in regulated or unusual niches, that control is worth the premium. For everyone else, we usually recommend Shopify.

The trap with WooCommerce is maintenance. WordPress needs updates — core, theme, plugins — and an ecommerce store cannot afford a broken update on a sale weekend. Budget $150–$600/month for managed maintenance, or accept that you will be the one debugging a plugin conflict at 2am. Our [guide to securing a website](/blog/how-to-secure-a-website-2026) covers the hardening side; the short version is that an unmaintained WooCommerce store is a breach waiting to happen, because it is the most attacked ecommerce platform on earth.

### 3. Headless and custom builds: the $20,000+ club

Headless commerce — a custom frontend (usually Next.js) talking to Shopify, BigCommerce or a commerce API on the backend — is the route we take when a client outgrows platform themes: sub-second page loads across massive catalogues, editorial-grade design, or app-like shopping experiences. Custom builds start around $20,000 and agency guides put the realistic band at $20,000–$150,000+.

Buy this only for reasons you can write down. "We need a product configurator with 40 interdependent options," "we run five regional storefronts from one catalogue," "our checkout conversion justifies a dedicated frontend team" — these are reasons. "We want something unique" is not a reason; it is a $60,000 way to discover that Shopify themes are unique enough. Every custom build also buys you a maintenance relationship: you now own code that needs a developer forever. Budget 15–25% of the build cost annually, minimum.

### 4. Wix, Squarespace, and the simple-site builders

For a store with under 30 products and no ambitions beyond "take orders", Wix or Squarespace at $30–$70/month is genuinely fine. An agency-assisted setup runs $2,000–$8,000. The ceiling is real, though: limited payment options in some regions, weaker SEO controls, and migration pain when you outgrow them. We see these as starter homes, not forever homes. If you plan to be doing $500,000 a year in three years, start on the platform you will still be on then.

### 5. Magento/Adobe Commerce and the enterprise tier

If you need to ask what it costs, you probably don't need it. Adobe Commerce builds start around $50,000 and enterprise projects run into the hundreds of thousands. It exists for companies with dedicated ecommerce teams and complex B2B requirements. For a founder reading a pricing guide, it is background noise — but know the name, because some agencies will quote it to make their Shopify price look reasonable. It isn't a comparable product.

## What actually moves your quote: the cost drivers

Two founders can both say "I need an online store" and get quotes $50,000 apart. Here is where the money hides.

### Payments and checkout complexity

One gateway in one currency is baseline. Every addition costs: a second gateway ($500–$2,000 to integrate and test properly), multi-currency pricing with localised checkout ($2,000–$8,000), cash-on-delivery workflows with order verification ($1,000–$3,000 — still the dominant payment method for Pakistani domestic retail, and it needs fraud controls or you will ship to ghosts), and subscription billing ($3,000–$10,000 to do well, because failed-payment recovery is a product in itself).

### Product data: the work nobody budgets for

Migrating or building a catalogue is the most underestimated line item in ecommerce. Clean data for 500 products — titles, descriptions, variants, images, SEO fields — is 80–150 hours of skilled work. At agency rates that is $4,000–$12,000, and it is the task most likely to be "handled internally" and then not handled at all. If your product data is a mess of supplier spreadsheets, say so upfront. An honest agency prices the cleanup; a cheap one discovers it mid-project and bills you for the delay.

### Integrations

The store is never the whole system. Email marketing and SMS ($500–$1,500 to wire properly), accounting and inventory sync ($1,000–$5,000), CRM connections ($1,000–$4,000), shipping carrier APIs with live rates ($800–$2,500 per carrier), and ERP or warehouse systems ($5,000–$25,000 — this is where "complex" tier pricing comes from). Each integration is also a future maintenance liability. We advise clients to launch with the minimum integrations that affect revenue and add the rest in phase two, when real order data tells you what matters.

### Design scope: theme vs custom

A premium theme with professional customisation: $2,000–$8,000 and it looks 90% as good as custom for most stores. Fully custom design: $8,000–$30,000+ and worth it only when brand differentiation directly drives the sale — fashion, beauty, premium food, anything where the site is the showroom. Our position is unfashionable but consistent: spend the design budget on product photography and the checkout flow before you spend it on a bespoke homepage. Nobody ever bought shoes because the hero section was avant-garde.

### Speed and technical SEO

A slow store is a tax on every marketing pound you spend. Core Web Vitals work on an ecommerce build — image pipelines, font discipline, checkout performance — runs $1,500–$6,000 as part of a build, and it pays for itself in ad efficiency. We wrote the full playbook in our [Core Web Vitals guide](/blog/core-web-vitals-2026); the ecommerce-specific point is that every 100ms of checkout latency measurably costs conversions, so this is not polish, it is plumbing.

### Compliance and data security

The 2026 budget guides flag this explicitly — [Dotsquares' October 2026 guide](https://www.dotsquares.com/press-and-events/tech/website-development-cost-guide-2026) notes that strict privacy and data-security compliance now adds real development and auditing hours to ecommerce builds: GDPR, CCPA and their cousins mean consent management, data handling, cookie compliance, PCI-adjacent checkout hygiene. Budget $1,000–$4,000 for a proper setup, more if you operate across the EU and California. Skipping it is not a saving; it is a fine with a delay timer.

## The US–Pakistan pricing math: why the same store costs 4x less

This is the part of the guide that matters most to our two audiences, so let's be blunt about it.

A competent Shopify build that costs $12,000–$18,000 from a US agency costs PKR 1,200,000–2,500,000 ($4,000–$8,500) from a good Pakistani studio. A custom headless build quoted at $80,000 in New York goes for PKR 5,000,000–8,000,000 ($18,000–$28,000) in Lahore or Karachi. The ratio holds across tiers: roughly 3x to 5x cheaper, for equivalent senior talent. The 2026 budget guides note the same pattern globally — offshore teams delivering equivalent expertise at far lower rates than London or New York agencies.

![Abstract illustration of a glowing globe connecting two distant studio desks, representing offshore collaboration between the US and Pakistan](/blog/ecommerce-website-cost-2026/fig2.webp)

### Why the gap exists (and why it isn't a quality gap)

It is mostly cost of living, not skill. A senior developer in Karachi earns a strong local salary at a fraction of a Bay Area equivalent. Pakistan's freelancer and agency ecosystem has also spent a decade serving US and UK clients, so the good studios already work in your timezone overlap, write in fluent English, and understand Western checkout conventions. The gap is real and it is not going away.

### When offshore wins

Well-scoped platform builds. If the requirements are clear — Shopify or WooCommerce, defined catalogue, standard integrations — a Pakistani studio will deliver the same store for a third of the US price. This is the highest-value transaction in the whole guide, and it is why half our client base is American.

### When it doesn't

Three situations where we tell US clients to hire locally or pay the premium: first, when the project needs daily in-person-style collaboration with a non-technical stakeholder — timezone friction is real, even with overlap hours. Second, when the store touches regulated US data or contracts that require domestic handling. Third, when the timeline is brutally short; a local team you can call at 9am your time is worth the markup when launch is in three weeks.

### How to buy offshore without getting burned

The price advantage disappears if you pick wrong. Our checklist, from years of cleaning up after bad offshore engagements: demand a live portfolio of stores you can click through and buy from (not screenshots); insist on a staging site you can review weekly; pay in milestones tied to working demos, never large upfront sums; confirm who exactly does the work — the senior you met or a junior you didn't; and get the maintenance terms in writing before the build starts, because a cheap build with no support is the most expensive kind. An agency that resists any of these is telling you everything.

One more honest note for Pakistani founders reading this: the same math works in reverse. If you are selling domestically, a Pakistani studio gives you local payment expertise (COD workflows, Easypaisa/JazzCash-adjacent thinking, Urdu-language UX instincts) that a US agency simply doesn't have. Don't overpay for a foreign badge on a local problem.

## The costs nobody puts in the proposal

The build quote is the down payment. Here is the rest of the bill, which is where cheap builds quietly become expensive ones.

**Apps and plugins.** A real Shopify store runs 5–12 paid apps: reviews, upsells, loyalty, email capture, currency conversion, search. At $10–$50 per app per month, that is $100–$400/month you didn't budget. WooCommerce extensions renew yearly and a serious store carries $500–$1,500/year in renewals. Audit the app stack quarterly. We regularly find stores paying for three apps that do the same job.

**Payment processing.** This dwarfs everything else at scale and nobody mentions it in the build quote. At 2.9% + 30¢ per transaction, a store doing $50,000/month pays ~$1,500/month in processing — more than the entire Shopify subscription. Negotiate rates once volume justifies it, and model this cost before you celebrate revenue milestones.

**Maintenance and updates.** Budget 15–25% of the build cost per year. For platform stores this means updates, monitoring, small improvements and emergency cover ($200–$800/month). For custom builds it means a developer relationship ($800–$3,000/month). A store with no maintenance budget is a store with a countdown timer.

**Content and catalogue operations.** Product photography, descriptions, seasonal campaigns, landing pages for sales events. Either you do this in-house (budget the staff time honestly) or you retain the studio ($500–$2,000/month). The stores that grow treat content as an operating cost, not a launch task.

**Redesign cycle.** Ecommerce design has a shelf life of 3–5 years. Platform shifts, brand evolution and conversion learning will force a rebuild. Amortise the build over four years when you evaluate the quote — a $20,000 store is $5,000/year, which reframes every pricing conversation.

## How to buy a store without getting burned

A short checklist to take into any agency conversation, ours included:

1. **Get the complexity tier in writing.** Simple, moderate or complex — which did they assume? If they can't answer, they haven't scoped it.
2. **Ask what is excluded.** Data entry, photography, copywriting, app subscriptions, ongoing fees. The exclusions list is more informative than the inclusions.
3. **Demand a staging site and weekly demos.** No black-box builds. You should see the store taking shape every week.
4. **Test checkout with real money before launch.** Real cards, real countries, real refunds. Every time. No exceptions.
5. **Get maintenance terms before the build starts.** Price, response times, what counts as a bug versus a feature.
6. **Own everything.** Domain, store account, code repository, analytics — registered to you, not the agency. Put it in the contract.
7. **Beware the two red flags.** A quote with no questions asked about your products or customers is a template sale. A timeline that sounds too good — "full custom store in two weeks" — is a lie or a disaster in progress.

The cheapest store is the one you only build once. Spend the scoping effort upfront; it is the highest-ROI hour in the entire project.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does an ecommerce website cost in 2026?

Expect a working online store to cost $5,000–$60,000 on Shopify or WooCommerce and $20,000–$150,000+ for a custom or headless build, based on 2026 agency pricing guides. In Pakistan, equivalent builds from a competent studio typically run PKR 600,000–6,000,000. A simple store with under 50 products, one payment gateway and a premium theme sits at the bottom of that range; multi-currency, integrations and custom features push it up fast.

### Is Shopify cheaper than WooCommerce?

Shopify is cheaper to launch: plans run $29–$299 per month on annual billing, and you can have a credible store live in weeks with almost no technical overhead. WooCommerce itself is free, but hosting, a premium theme, paid plugins and developer time mean a professionally built WooCommerce store usually costs more upfront than a comparable Shopify store. The trade-off is ownership: WooCommerce has no monthly platform fee and no per-transaction surcharge beyond the payment processor's cut.

### What is the cheapest way to build an ecommerce website?

A DIY Shopify store with a free theme and one payment gateway is the cheapest credible route — roughly $800 covers a year of Shopify Basic on annual billing plus a domain and a couple of paid apps, per Shopify's own 2026 cost guide. It works for validating demand. Once revenue matters, the hidden costs appear: apps, payment fees, theme customisation and conversion work usually outweigh the subscription itself.

### Why do ecommerce quotes vary so much?

Quotes vary because "an ecommerce website" describes everything from a 20-product store to a multi-currency marketplace. The GoodFirms 2026 survey of 300+ web firms found fixed-price web projects landing anywhere from $1,000 to $150,000+, with 63% of agencies quoting between $1,000 and $15,000. Your position in that spread is decided by product count, payment complexity, integrations, custom features, design scope and who is doing the building.

### How much does an ecommerce website cost in Pakistan?

Pakistani studios typically build ecommerce sites for PKR 600,000–6,000,000 depending on scope — a Shopify store at the lower end, a custom or headless build at the top. A freelancer may go lower, but at agency level you are paying for proper payment-gateway integration, checkout testing, speed work and ongoing support. US agencies charge $15,000–$150,000+ for equivalent work, which is why Pakistani founders often buy offshore and US founders sometimes hire Pakistani teams.

### What are the ongoing costs of running an ecommerce website?

Plan for 15–25% of the build cost per year: platform subscriptions, hosting, domain renewals, SSL, paid apps or plugins, payment processing fees (typically 2–3% per transaction), and maintenance. A store doing serious revenue also needs a monthly retainer for updates, security patches and conversion optimisation — budget $200–$2,000 per month depending on size.

### Should I build a custom ecommerce website or use Shopify?

Use Shopify (or WooCommerce) unless you have a reason you can name. Custom builds start around $20,000 and the reasons that justify them are specific: complex product configurators, multi-vendor marketplaces, subscription logic that platforms handle badly, or integrations with legacy ERP systems. If your store just sells products with standard variants, a custom build buys you bragging rights and maintenance bills, not revenue.

### How long does it take to build an ecommerce website?

A Shopify store with a premium theme: 2–4 weeks. A WooCommerce store with custom design: 6–10 weeks. A custom or headless ecommerce build: 3–6 months. Add 2–4 weeks to each if you are migrating from an old platform, because data cleanup — product catalogues, customer records, redirect maps — always takes longer than anyone estimates.
