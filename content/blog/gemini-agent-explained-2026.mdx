Google had a busy week. On Thursday, the company's cloud division announced the Gemini Agent — a single AI agent for work that takes a goal, goes off and does the work, and comes back with the finished result. Your next coworker might have an email address, a calendar, a Drive folder, and no pulse.

## What the Gemini Agent actually is

Strip away the launch-day gloss and the Gemini Agent is Google's answer to a simple observation: people don't want a smarter chatbot. They want work done.

The agent plans the work, uses tools, connects to a company's business systems, and returns finished work in documents, email and developer environments. You write something like "prepare the Q4 supplier review pack," close your laptop, and the agent keeps running in the cloud — hours, even days — then delivers a finished document. It works directly inside Gmail, Docs, Sheets, Slides, Drive, Chat and Calendar, and — in a move that raised eyebrows — also reaches into Microsoft 365 and Slack.

Connectors matter here more than the model. The agent plugs into Salesforce, ServiceNow, Jira, Snowflake, BigQuery and anything that speaks Model Context Protocol, the open standard that acts as a universal plug between AI and software. An agent that cannot touch your CRM is a demo. An agent that can read your pipeline, draft the follow-ups, and log them back in is infrastructure.

Our take: the persistent part is the real product. Assistants reset every conversation; this thing keeps session, semantic, procedural and episodic memory, so it remembers business context when you switch devices or pause an assignment. That is the difference between a tool and a team member. It is also, frankly, where things can go wrong — an agent with long memory and write access to your systems needs adult supervision. More on that below.

The timing is not accidental. Google Cloud CEO Thomas Kurian said at the launch event that "work now starts in the prompt window." Translation: Google sees the prompt box replacing the inbox as the front door of office software, and it intends to own the front door.

## Coworker agents: the part that changes team structure

The feature that will cause the most hallway conversation is what Google calls coworker agents. A manager describes a role — say, events coordinator or research assistant — and Gemini creates an agent with its own Workspace account: its own email address, calendar, Drive storage and a listing in the company directory.

Read that again. Your AI helper doesn't live in a side panel. It sits in your directory next to the actual humans, sends emails from its own address, books rooms on its own calendar, and stores files in its own Drive. Each agent only gets access to the information it is given, and — this is the part enterprises asked for — every action lands in an audit trail attributed to the agent rather than a person. Google gives each agent a cryptographically verified identity, a digital ID checked through cryptography, so you can tell an agent's actions apart from an employee's.

![Abstract illustration of digital AI coworker agents joining a team meeting in a modern office](/blog/gemini-agent-explained-2026/fig1.webp)

Why does this matter more than it sounds? Because it solves the identity problem that has quietly blocked agent adoption in real companies. Up until now, agents acted *as you* — burning your credentials, your permissions, your audit history. Nobody in compliance liked that. Giving agents their own identity, their own permissions and their own audit trail turns an agent from a liability into something a security team can approve. If you are weighing [how to deploy AI agents in your business](/blog/deploy-ai-agents-in-business-2026), this single design decision is the one to watch: agent identity is what makes agents deployable at scale.

The trade-off is cultural, not technical. Managers will now write job descriptions for software. Headcount planning gets weird. And the question "who approved that email?" gets a genuinely new answer: the agent did, acting within the role it was given. Companies that figure out role design for agents — what each one may do, what needs a human sign-off, who reviews the audit trail — will move faster than companies that treat them as fancy macros.

Google is starting with versions for financial services and legal work in preview, with government, healthcare and retail versions on the way. That sequencing tells you everything: regulated industries first, because that is where audit trails and identity controls are the whole ballgame.
## Why Google is shipping its rival's models inside its own product

Here is the sentence that would have been unthinkable two years ago: the Gemini Agent currently runs on Google's Gemini models **and Anthropic's Claude models**, with more models to be added later.

Google — the company with one of the best model labs on the planet — is putting a competitor's model inside its flagship workplace product. Why? Because Google has decided the real prize isn't the model. It is the directory, the identity system, the connectors, the audit trail. Whoever controls *where work happens* matters more than whoever writes the smartest next token. Claude runs the task when it is the better tool for the job; Gemini runs it otherwise. A smart-routing layer picks per task, balancing quality against cost.

This is genuinely good news for buyers. It means the agent platform competes on orchestration, not lock-in. You are not betting on one model family for the next five years; you are buying a routing layer that can swap models as the leaderboard changes. For the cost-conscious, it also introduces hard project-level spending limits and committed-use savings plans — 10% off for a one-year commitment, 20% for three years. Agent workloads can spiral quietly when every task is a hundred API calls, so the spending caps are the feature finance teams should ask about first.

For our money, this validates a pattern we've been telling clients all year: the model is a commodity, the workflow is the asset. If you are building on one model provider with no abstraction layer, the October 2026 news cycle should make you nervous.

## What will go wrong: the failure modes to plan for

We work with agents in production, so let us say the quiet part out loud. Everything in the launch announcement is the happy path. Here is what the first year of coworker agents will actually look like inside real companies, and what to do about each one.

**Confident mistakes at machine speed.** An agent with write access to your CRM and your inbox will eventually do the wrong thing quickly and at scale — send the follow-up to the wrong segment, update the wrong record, book the wrong meeting. Humans make these mistakes too, but humans make them one at a time. The fix is boring and non-negotiable: approval gates on every consequential action. Drafts are fine to automate; sends, writes and payments get a human click. Google says the agent asks for approval before consequential actions; make sure your policy actually enforces that instead of waving it through.

**Stale memory.** These agents remember — session, semantic, procedural, episodic. That is the selling point, and it is also a landmine. An agent acting in October on supplier terms it learned in March, or on a pricing sheet that changed last week, produces work that looks right and is wrong. The fix: treat agent memory like a database with a refresh policy, not like a colleague's judgment. Anything time-sensitive gets re-verified against the source system before it leaves the building.

**Permission sprawl.** Connectors to Salesforce, Jira, Snowflake and everything with an MCP plug sound wonderful until you realise each connector is a permission surface. Give every coworker agent the keys to the kingdom and you have built the world's most efficient insider threat. The principle is least privilege per agent: the research agent reads the web and your docs, but it does not touch the CRM. The sales agent touches the CRM, but it does not touch finance. Role design is access design.

**Invoice shock.** A single "prepare the Q4 review pack" task can chain dozens of tool calls and model invocations, each metered. Multiply by a team of agents running daily and the bill stops looking like software and starts looking like payroll. Set the project-level spending limits Google provides on day one, review usage weekly for the first quarter, and treat the committed-use discounts as a year-two conversation, not a launch-day decision.

**Accountability theatre.** The audit trail is the most important feature in this whole launch — and the easiest to waste. An audit log nobody reads is decoration. Somebody in your company needs the job of reviewing agent actions: a weekly fifteen-minute scan of what the agents did, what they touched, what got flagged. Without that human in the loop, you have not bought governance. You have bought the appearance of it.

None of this is a reason to sit out. It is a reason to pilot with your eyes open. Agents fail in predictable ways, which means the failures are manageable — if you design for them up front instead of discovering them in a post-mortem.

If an agent on a $14 seat (about PKR 3,900) absorbs even half of it, the maths clears by a factor of two. ## What business owners should do this month

Not adopt it. Not yet. Here is the four-step move we recommend to clients, in order:

**1. Audit what you already pay for.** Most ten-person companies we talk to pay for three overlapping AI subscriptions — a ChatGPT team plan, a Copilot seat or two, some Gemini add-on nobody remembers buying — and use none of them properly. Kill the duplicates before you add a fourth. That audit usually pays for itself in a month.

**2. Pick one repeatable workflow and run a four-week pilot.** Inbox triage. Meeting notes to action items. Lead follow-up drafting. Pick the one that eats the most staff hours, give one platform a single workflow, and measure: hours saved, errors caught, time to output. If you cannot measure it, you cannot justify it — and "it feels faster" is how AI budgets die.

**3. Learn the governance controls before you scale.** Agent identity, audit trails, spending limits, human approval for consequential actions. Read our [AI agent safety playbook](/blog/ai-agent-governance-safety-playbook-2026) if you haven't. The companies that get burned by agents in 2027 will be the ones that skipped this step in 2026.

**4. Wait for pricing to settle before committing a department.** The 10–20% committed-use discounts are bait. Previews become products, prices move, competitors respond. Run monthly, decide quarterly.

And the one thing to start this week: check your Workspace admin console. Agent features land there silently, and the admin who discovers them first gets to set the policy. The admin who discovers them last gets to clean up the mess.

## This week in web tech: the rest of the news that affects your business

The Gemini Agent was the headline, but three other stories from the past week land closer to home for anyone running a website.

### Chrome is going HTTPS-by-default this month

Starting with Chrome 154, released this month, the browser enables "Always Use Secure Connections" for everyone. Chrome will attempt every connection over HTTPS first, and it will ask for your permission before loading any public site that doesn't support it.

For most sites this changes nothing — over 95% of Chrome page loads already use HTTPS. But if any part of your web presence still serves over plain HTTP — a legacy subdomain, an old landing page, a staging link that leaked into Google — your visitors will now hit a warning screen. That is a trust killer and a conversion killer.

Fix it this week: audit every property you own for HTTP connections, put certificates on everything public, and test with the setting switched on in Chrome today. If your site is on our stack, this is a [service](/services) we handle routinely — but any host can issue a free certificate in minutes. There is no excuse left for HTTP in October 2026.

### Microsoft bets $2,500 on the AI PC

Microsoft unveiled the Surface Ultra this week — a laptop starting above $2,500 (roughly PKR 700,000) built around Nvidia's RTX Spark AI chip, with Satya Nadella and Jensen Huang sharing a stage to sell a "hybrid intelligence" future: AI that runs on your device instead of in a data centre.

Our read: the hardware is not the story. The story is that every platform vendor now agrees AI workloads are splitting — some in the cloud, some on the device — and software should stop caring which. For businesses, that means the next generation of your team's laptops will run agents locally: faster, offline-capable, and cheaper per task than cloud calls. If you refresh hardware on a three-year cycle, the machines you buy in 2027 will be agent-native. Plan your software budget accordingly.

### Kagi gives up on Orion for Windows and Linux — and open-sources it

Smaller story, bigger lesson. Kagi announced it is ending development of its Orion browser for Windows and Linux and handing the code to the community, focusing instead on macOS and iOS. Orion is built on Apple's WebKit rather than Chromium, and porting WebKit to other platforms turned out to be a maintenance nightmare for a small team.

The lesson for founders: platform bets are strategy, not preference. Kagi chose focus over sprawl, and that is the right call for a small team — spreading thin across platforms is how good products die slowly.
