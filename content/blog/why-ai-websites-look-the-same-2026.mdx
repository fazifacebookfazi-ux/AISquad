Open ten AI-built landing pages and you will see the same page ten times. A centred hero with a purple-to-blue gradient. Inter at semibold. Three feature cards with rounded corners and a soft shadow. A testimonial strip. A dark footer. None of it is wrong. All of it is interchangeable — and your visitors can tell in half a second that nobody made a single deliberate visual decision.

We see this every week now. A founder in Lahore or Austin ships a site with Lovable, v0, Bolt or Cursor in an evening, shows it to us proudly, and asks why it is not converting. The site works. It loads. It is responsive. And it looks like ten thousand other products, because that is precisely what it is: the statistical average of every landing page the model has ever seen.

This is not a rant about AI tools. We use them daily, and we like them. This is a field guide to the one problem they cannot solve for you — taste — and the practical process that fixes it. The fix is not a better prompt. The fix is making the decisions before the model does.

## The tell: spot an AI-default site in five seconds

There is a quick test we run in first calls. We screenshot a client's homepage, strip the logo, and ask: could this be any of your competitors? If the answer is yes — and for AI-built sites it almost always is — the site has a sameness problem, not a quality problem.

The [tells](https://www.925studios.co/blog/ai-slop-design-tells) are consistent enough to list:

- **The typeface.** Inter, almost always. It is a fine typeface. It is also the default in half the tools and the most common font on the modern web, which makes it the visual equivalent of a shrug.
- **The gradient.** Indigo to purple, top-left to bottom-right, behind the hero. Tailwind's creator has publicly regretted making indigo-500 the default that every AI tool now copies.
- **The three cards.** Three features, three pricing tiers, three testimonials. The model loves threes because threes dominate its training data.
- **The hero formula.** Big headline, subheadline, two buttons, dashboard mockup tilted at a slight angle, "trusted by" bar of greyed-out logos underneath.
- **The voice.** Headlines that promise to "supercharge", "streamline" or "unlock" something, written by a model that has read a million SaaS pages and averaged them all.

Here is the uncomfortable part: none of this looks broken. A generic site does not bounce visitors because it is ugly. It bounces them because it is unmemorable. When a buyer opens five tabs to compare vendors and four of them look identical, the fifth one — the one with a point of view — wins the callback. Generic is worse than ugly, because ugly at least gets remembered.

## Why models output the average

This is mechanics, not mystery. A language model does not design. It predicts the most probable next token, pixel or pattern given what you asked for. When your brief contains zero design decisions, the model fills every gap with the most frequent pattern in its data. The average is not a bug in the tool. It is what the tool is.

Three forces stack up to make the average so sticky:

**1. The training data is itself repetitive.** The modern web converged on a narrow set of patterns years ago — the SaaS hero, the card grid, the dashboard mockup — and models learned that convergence as if it were good taste. A [2025 study](https://dev.to/adioof/ai-generated-websites-are-the-word-art-era-all-over-again-42j0) cited in design press this year found more than 80% of AI-generated websites sharing almost the same underlying structure. The model is not copying one site. It is copying the mean of all of them.

**2. The tools ship with the same defaults.** The frameworks, component libraries and templates that AI tools reach for share a palette, a type scale and a spacing system. When every tool starts from the same starter kit, every output starts from the same look, no matter which model you pick.

**3. Vague briefs leave no room for anything else.** "Make me a landing page for my SaaS" names a category, not a design direction. It says nothing about who uses the product, what they need to do first, what should matter most on the page, or what visual character fits the brand. Words like "clean", "modern" and "professional" do not solve this — they describe broad preferences, not choices a designer or a model can apply. A useful brief replaces them with observable direction: a warm editorial palette, a compact type hierarchy, generous space around the main action, a dense data view for someone checking metrics at a glance.

The October 2026 research on this is bracing. Designer Fund and Foundation Capital's [AI in Design Report 2026](https://research.breon.ai/), covering more than 900 designers in 60 countries, puts weekly AI use at 91%, up from 54% a year earlier, with 75% using AI daily. Adoption is near-universal. But Figma's own researchers ran a randomised trial — 100 participants, half product designers and half product managers, three standardised UI tasks with and without Figma Make — and found product managers finished 35% faster while designers showed no significant aggregate time saving. Their one real gain was building an interaction prototype, 26% faster.

Read that result carefully. The leverage of these tools is in widening who can produce UI, not in speeding up people who already could. That is why your competitor's intern can now ship a site that looks like yours: the tool flattened the floor, not the ceiling. The ceiling is still taste, and taste still has to come from somewhere.

## The real cost of looking like everyone else

Founders usually frame this as an aesthetic worry. It is a commercial one. Three costs show up in our client work again and again:

**Trust leaks first.** People judge credibility from design before they read a word. A site that looks templated reads as a business that cut corners, even when the business behind it is solid. For a Pakistani software house selling to US clients, or a US startup selling to cautious buyers, that first impression is the whole pitch. A generic site asks the visitor to trust you despite the evidence of their eyes.

**Comparison becomes impossible to win.** If your site and your three closest competitors share the same layout, the same gradient and the same three cards, the visitor's decision collapses to price. You did not choose to compete on price. Your design chose for you.

**Search and AI answers ignore you.** This one is newer and it matters more every month. Search systems and AI answer engines reward distinctiveness: first-hand experience, specific claims, original structure. A page that mirrors everyone else's structure and wording gives them nothing unique to cite. And the sameness rarely stops at design — the same process that produces a generic hero usually produces generic copy, the same three features, the same vague claims. Fixing the design usually forces the content to get specific too, which is where the SEO benefit actually comes from.

There is a fourth cost nobody talks about: your own team stops believing in the site. We have watched founders apologise for their homepage on sales calls. A site you are slightly embarrassed by does not get shared, linked or demoed with pride. That quiet drag is hard to measure and easy to feel.

## The fix starts before the prompt

Everything that follows rests on one principle: **decide first, generate second.** The model is excellent at executing decisions and terrible at making them. Your job is to hand it a brief so specific that the average cannot survive contact with it.

Here is the decision set we write down before any tool opens, for every project:

- **One sentence about the buyer.** Not a persona deck — one sentence. "A US clinic owner who has been burned by an agency before and needs proof before a call." Every later decision gets tested against that sentence.
- **The single action the page exists for.** One action. Book a call, start a trial, request a quote. Everything that does not serve it gets cut or demoted.
- **A type pairing with a reason.** Not "a nice font" — a pairing chosen for a reason you can state. A serif display face for an editorial, trustworthy feel; a grotesque for a technical, dense product. The reason matters more than the font, because the reason survives when the model suggests swapping it.
- **A colour system, not a colour.** Three roles: a dominant neutral, one accent with a job (calls to action only), and one expressive colour for brand moments. If your accent appears in forty places, it has no job.
- **A spacing and radius rule.** Tight or generous? Sharp or soft? Pick one end of each scale and hold it everywhere. Consistency here is what makes a site feel designed rather than assembled.
- **Three things you will never do.** Ours usually include: no purple gradients, no three-identical-cards rows, no stock handshake photography. A "never" list is the fastest way to kill the average, because the average is made of exactly these defaults.
- **Real content, not lorem ipsum.** Real headlines, real numbers, real customer language. A model given placeholder text will invent generic claims to fill the space, and those claims will ship if nobody checks.

Write this down in a document, not in your head. One page is enough. This document is the most valuable artefact in the whole project, because it is the thing every later step — human or machine — gets measured against.

## October 2026 changed the tooling: give the model the rulebook

Until recently, the advice above lived in a Google Doc the model never saw. That changed on 15 October 2026, when Figma [announced Specs](https://www.analyticsinsight.net/news/figma-gives-ai-agents-a-design-rulebook-with-new-specs-feature) at Config India in Bengaluru — its first Config event in India, drawing more than 30,000 registrations. Specs are Markdown documents that live beside your designs and spell out your design language in plain text: the rules, the guidelines, the reasoning behind the brand's visual identity.

The point that matters for this article: Specs are written to be read by AI agents as well as humans. You can connect them to existing designs, diagrams and code, so an agent starts from your rules instead of from the internet's average. Figma also moved Code Layers into open beta at the same event — working code on the canvas next to the design — and added Payload as a backend path for Figma Make projects, with a CMS, database, authentication and file storage inside the same workflow.

We have written about [Figma Specs](/blog/figma-specs-explained-2026) and [Code Layers](/blog/figma-code-layers-open-beta-2026) in detail already, so here is the short version of what it means for a founder: the industry's biggest design tool just admitted that AI output needs a rulebook, and built the rulebook into the product. The era of "just prompt it and see" is officially over. The teams that will look distinct in 2027 are the ones writing their rules down now.

You do not need Figma to do this. A Markdown file in your repo, a Notion page, or a section in your brand guide works the same way, as long as it is specific and the model can read it. Figma Specs just makes it harder to skip. If your agency or freelancer cannot show you the document your site's AI output was measured against, they do not have one.

## A studio process that breaks the average

Here is the process we actually run when a client arrives with a generic AI-built site, or asks us to build one that will not look generic. It takes longer than an evening. It takes less time than you think.

**Step 1: The teardown (half a day).** We screenshot the current site and five competitors, strip the logos, and lay them side by side. Anything that appears in three or more screenshots is a default, not a decision, and it goes on the kill list. This exercise alone convinces most founders, because seeing your site next to four identical ones ends the argument about whether the problem is real.

**Step 2: The decision document (one day).** The seven decisions from the previous section, written down and signed off by the founder. This is the step clients resist — it feels like homework — and it is the step that determines everything after it. A founder who will not decide what their brand stands for will get the average, no matter who builds the site.

**Step 3: Direction before screens (one to two days).** We produce three visual directions, not one finished homepage. Each direction is a single screen: type, colour, spacing, one real headline. The founder picks a direction or kills all three and we go again. This is deliberately cheap and fast, because choosing a direction after fifty screens are built is how projects die.

**Step 4: Build with the model on a leash (one to two weeks).** Now the AI tools earn their keep. We generate screens inside the chosen direction, with the decision document and the Specs file as context, and we review every output against the document. Anything that drifts back toward the average — a purple gradient sneaking in, a three-card row appearing uninvited — gets corrected and the correction gets added to the rulebook so it does not recur. The model does the production work; the document does the taste work.

**Step 5: The blind test (half a day).** We strip the logo from the finished homepage and show it to five people who have never seen the brand. If they can describe what kind of company it is and what makes it different, the site passes. If they say "a tech company, I guess", it goes back. This test is brutal and it is the reason our sites do not look like each other.

Two things to notice about this process. First, AI is doing most of the production labour in step 4 — we are not romantics about hand-crafting every pixel. Second, the expensive steps are the human ones: deciding and judging. That is the honest division of labour in 2026. The model builds; you decide; the document remembers.

## What to keep from AI, and what to take back

Not everything the model produces is slop. Some of it is genuinely good, and throwing it all away would be as foolish as accepting all of it. Here is our working split:

**Keep from the model:** scaffolding and layout speed, variations (ask for six hero options and keep one), responsive behaviour, boilerplate code, accessibility basics like semantic structure and focus states, and first-draft copy that a human then rewrites with real specifics.

**Take back from the model:** the type pairing, the colour system, the spacing language, the headline claims, the imagery direction, the information architecture (what goes where and why), and every decision that answers "what makes us different". If the model made it and you cannot explain why it is there, it belongs in the second list.

A useful rule of thumb: if you can swap your logo for a competitor's and nothing else on the page feels wrong, the model still owns your design. Your job is to reach the point where swapping the logo would feel absurd — because the type, the colour, the spacing and the words could only belong to you.

There is also a performance angle worth checking while you are in there. AI-generated code is often bloated: unused components, heavy scripts, unoptimised images. We regularly see AI-built pages shipping two to three times the JavaScript they need, which hurts load times and Core Web Vitals. A de-generic pass is a good moment to audit that too — the same review that fixes the look usually fixes the weight.

## The redesign test: would you recognise your own site blind?

Try this tonight, before you spend anything. Open your homepage, cover the logo with your hand, and read the page as a stranger. Ask three questions:

1. What does this company sell, in one sentence?
2. Who is it for, specifically?
3. What is one thing here I could not find on a competitor's site?

If you struggle with any of the three, your site has a sameness problem. Most founders struggle with the third. That is normal — it is the question the average cannot answer, because the average has no answer to give.

The fix for question three is rarely visual. It is usually a specificity problem: a real number, a real customer story, a real opinion, a real guarantee. "We ship in 3 weeks" beats "we deliver excellence". "Built for clinics with 2–10 locations" beats "healthcare solutions". Specificity is the cheapest differentiator on the internet, and it is the one thing a model cannot invent for you — because it does not know your business.

## Where this goes next

The sameness problem is about to get more interesting, not less. Three shifts are already visible:

**Design systems are becoming machine-readable by default.** Figma's October announcements point one way: systems documented as structured text that agents can consume, validate and build from. The design system stops being a PDF nobody opens and becomes an API the whole toolchain reads. Teams that write their rules down now will have a head start that compounds, because every future AI output in their organisation will inherit those rules.

**Interfaces are becoming outcome-oriented.** Nielsen Norman Group's 2026 work on outcome-oriented design describes a shift from designing single screens to defining adaptive frameworks that respond to individual user goals rather than optimising for average users. The irony is sharp: the industry spent years optimising for the average user, and AI just automated the average screen. The next advantage goes to teams that design for specific outcomes and specific people — the exact thing the average cannot do.

**The human job moves up the stack.** Designer Fund's report found 65% of designers saying they take on more product or engineering work, while only 28% of leaders say their companies have updated evaluation, pay or hiring to match. The scope grew; the ladder did not. Expect that gap to close messily over the next year, with job titles and rates catching up to the reality that [a designer's value is now judgement, not production](https://blog.logrocket.com/ux-design/balancing-ai-in-ux-design/).

None of this means the end of generic design. It means generic design becomes free, instant and everywhere — which is precisely why it stops being a strategy. When everyone can produce a competent site in an evening, competence stops differentiating. Decisions differentiate.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do AI-generated websites all look the same?

Because a model fills in every decision you did not make with the statistical average of its training data. A prompt like "make me a SaaS landing page" contains no brand decisions, so the model reaches for the most common patterns it has seen: Inter, a purple-to-blue gradient, three rounded cards, a centred hero. The output is competent because the average is competent — and forgettable for exactly the same reason.

### What is AI slop design?

AI slop design is the generic look produced when AI tools are left to make taste decisions on their own: the Inter typeface, indigo-to-purple gradients, three feature cards in a row, rounded corners, a dark footer. None of those elements is bad on its own. The problem is that they are not choices at all — they are defaults, and your visitors have seen the same defaults on dozens of other sites this week.

### Can you fix a generic AI website without rebuilding it?

Usually, yes. The structure underneath is often fine; what is missing is a layer of deliberate decisions. In our experience, a re-skin pass — new type pairing, a real colour system derived from your brand, custom spacing and radius rules, rewritten headlines and real photography or illustration — changes how a site reads within a week of work. A full rebuild is only needed when the AI output also has structural problems: bloated code, slow load times, or a broken information architecture.

### What are Figma Specs and how do they help?

Figma Specs, announced at Config India on 15 October 2026, are Markdown documents that sit beside your designs and spell out your design language in plain text — rules a human designer and an AI agent can both read. Instead of hoping the model guesses your brand, you hand it the rulebook: which typefaces, which colours mean what, how cards behave, what never to do. It is the single most practical fix for generic AI output to arrive this year.

### Do visitors actually notice generic AI design?

Yes, and faster than you would like. People cannot always name what is wrong, but they categorise a generic-looking site as low-budget within seconds — the same instinct that used to flag stock handshake photos and clip art. The cost is not embarrassment; it is trust. A visitor comparing five similar vendors will remember the one that looked deliberate and forget the four that looked interchangeable.

### Does generic AI design hurt SEO?

Indirectly, but really. Search systems and AI answer engines increasingly reward distinctiveness, first-hand experience and genuine expertise. A page that mirrors everyone else's structure and wording gives them nothing unique to cite or rank. The design sameness usually travels with content sameness — the same headlines, the same three features, the same claims — and that is what costs you visibility.

### How much does it cost to de-generic an AI-built website?

Based on our studio's observed range: a re-skin pass on an existing AI-built site runs $800–$2,000 (roughly PKR 225,000–560,000). A full brand-and-design-system pass — type, colour, components, motion rules and documentation an AI agent can follow — runs $2,500–$6,000 (roughly PKR 700,000–1,700,000). A complete redesign with new information architecture starts around $4,000 (roughly PKR 1,100,000).

### Should I still use AI tools to build my website?

Yes — for the parts where speed beats taste. AI tools are superb at scaffolding, first drafts, variations and production work once the decisions exist. The mistake is asking the tool to make the decisions. Keep the model on a short leash: give it a written brief, a design system and a rulebook, then review its output against those documents. Used that way, AI is the fastest junior designer you have ever hired.

Your website does not need to be the most beautiful site on the internet. It needs to be the one a visitor remembers after closing five tabs. That is a much lower bar than most founders think, and a much more specific one: one real type pairing, one colour system with jobs, one clear action, and a handful of specifics no competitor can copy because they are yours.

If your site currently fails the blind test — logo covered, stranger reading, third question unanswered — that is not a verdict on your business. It is a verdict on a process that skipped the decisions. The decisions take a day to write down. The model will happily build the rest.
