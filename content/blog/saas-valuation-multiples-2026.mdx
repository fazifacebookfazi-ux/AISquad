If you are a SaaS founder, the number you quote at the start of 2026 is probably wrong by now. We have watched this happen in real time: a founder walks into a funding conversation with a 2021-era multiple in mind, the term sheet lands 40% below it, and they spend three weeks insisting the investor is lowballing. The investor is not lowballing. The market moved, and most founders only found out this week.

On 30 September 2026, Forvis Mazars and PitchBook published their [H1 2026 State of SaaS report](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/09/30/3372082/0/en/forvis-mazars-and-pitchbook-release-h1-2026-state-of-saas-report.html), and the headline figures should be required reading for anyone building software. Median SaaS enterprise-value-to-EBITDA multiples for private equity deals fell to **11.7x from 20.4x** — a reset that nearly erases the premium SaaS commanded over ordinary software for a decade. AI-driven strategic acquisitions pushed global SaaS M&A value to a decade-high **$439.7 billion**, while only **23 SaaS companies went public** in the first half, and exits outside a handful of outliers stayed muted.

Translation for founders: the money is still there, but it has become picky. It pays up for profitable growth and for AI that genuinely drives revenue, and it discounts everything else. This guide explains what buyers actually pay now, why the old numbers are dead, and what you can do in the next 90 days to move your multiple up before you raise, sell, or even start the conversation.

## The numbers that just reset the market

Let us start with the cold facts, because everything else in this article hangs off them.

The Forvis Mazars–PitchBook report put four structural shifts on the record. First, dealmaking has concentrated: that $439.7 billion in global SaaS M&A value is a decade high, driven by AI-fuelled strategic acquisitions — big incumbents buying AI capability rather than building it. Second, valuations reset: median EV/EBITDA fell from 20.4x to 11.7x as AI disruption risk and a higher cost of capital killed the old SaaS premium. Third, exits underdelivered: global SaaS PE and VC exit volumes slowed to an estimated 1,458 in H1 2026 versus 1,652 in full-year 2025, and the aggregate figures flatter the reality — outside outlier deals, the exit landscape is quiet. Fourth, the IPO window barely opened: 23 SaaS IPOs in six months, with mixed post-listing performance reminding everyone that public investors are far less forgiving than private ones.

On top of the PE numbers, the revenue-multiple picture from [independent multiple trackers](https://windsordrake.com/saas-valuation-multiples/) tells the same story at a lower multiple. Median SaaS M&A transactions priced at roughly **4.0x enterprise value to trailing-twelve-month revenue** in Q2 2026, and the median public SaaS company traded at **3.2x** across the 106-company SEG index. Private lower-middle-market SaaS valuations have stabilised around **4.0x to 5.5x ARR**. That is not a dip. That is the new level.

If you last raised in 2021 or 2022, sit down for this next bit: you may be holding an internal valuation, a 409A, or a liquidation preference stack built on numbers that no longer exist. We have seen founders discover this at the worst possible moment — during a down-round bridge, an acqui-hire conversation, or a co-founder buyout. Better to hear it here, calmly, than across a term sheet.

## Why the SaaS premium died

Three forces killed the premium, and none of them is reversing soon.

**Interest rates repriced everything.** Software valuations were the clearest beneficiary of the zero-rate era. When money costs nothing, a dollar of profit in 2035 is worth almost as much as a dollar today, and buyers happily paid 20x EBITDA for recurring revenue. [QuantPillar's private-market data](https://quantpillar.com/resources/guides/valuation-multiples/) makes the arithmetic explicit: a 2.5 percentage-point rise in the federal funds rate knocks roughly 25% off EBITDA multiples for debt-free businesses — more for leveraged deals. With rates holding in the 4.5% to 5.5% range, the era of multiple expansion doing the heavy lifting in PE returns is over. The multiple you get now has to be earned by the business itself, not the macro backdrop.

**AI made buyers discount defensibility.** This one stings. The same technology wave that lifts AI-native products punishes ordinary SaaS. When a buyer looks at a $10M-ARR workflow tool today, they are quietly modelling what happens if a competent team wraps an agent around the same workflow for a tenth of the price. PitchBook's data shows this explicitly: AI disruption risk is cited alongside cost of capital as the reason SaaS multiples converged with non-SaaS multiples. We tell our clients bluntly — if your product's moat is "a clean UI and decent integrations," that moat is now roughly as deep as a puddle. Switching costs, proprietary data, regulatory moats and embedded workflows still count. Pixels do not.

**Profitability became the admission ticket.** The report's quote from Forvis Mazars partner Ricardo Martinez is worth reading twice: investors now place "greater emphasis on profitability, cash flow, and competitive differentiation." Growth-at-all-costs companies that could raise at 15x revenue in 2021 are the ones trading at 4x today, because buyers no longer believe someone else will fund the burn later. The market's message is crude but consistent: grow fast *and* show the profit is real.

## What buyers actually pay in 2026

Strip away the commentary and this is the pricing grid. These are ranges our studio uses when advising founders, drawn from the H1 2026 report data and current multiple trackers, and they match what we see in live processes.

| Company profile | Typical multiple (2026) | What earns the top end |
|---|---|---|

Read the grid honestly. If you are a $3M-ARR company growing 25% with 15% churn and a chatbot bolted onto a dashboard, you are not in the top row. You are probably in the third row, negotiating down. The founders who command the top end of any band share one trait: their numbers are clean, verifiable, and improving. Messy books, recognised-revenue games, and a spreadsheet that reconciles "approximately" are how 6x becomes 4x before diligence is even finished.

One more uncomfortable truth from the data: median multiples are medians. Top-quartile disclosed deals still clear **above 8x ARR**. The spread between a well-run company and an average one is wider than it was in 2021. Excellence gets paid; average gets discounted. That is the market working as intended.

## The Rule of 40, operator-style

Every buyer, investor and acquirer in 2026 runs the same mental arithmetic within the first five minutes: **growth rate plus EBITDA margin — is it 40 or above?** A company growing 30% with a 10% margin scores 40. One growing 15% with a 25% margin also scores 40. Both signal the same thing: you know how to scale efficiently, not just how to sell.

![An abstract illustration of the Rule of 40: a brass balance scale weighing growth against profit in a deep teal space](/blog/saas-valuation-multiples-2026/fig1.webp)

Why does this one number matter so much? Because it compresses the growth-versus-profitability debate into a single quality filter, and because the pricing data backs it up. Companies clearing 40 consistently trade at **one to two turns of ARR above** otherwise comparable peers. The [SaaS Rise liquidity playbook's data](https://www.saasrise.com/blog/the-founder-liquidity-playbook-for-saas-ai) shows Rule of 40 companies pricing at roughly a **4.5x revenue multiple**, with the premium accelerating as the score rises. Below 40, every point you are short is a discount buyers can justify.

The version founders get wrong is treating it as a vanity stat. We watched a founder boast about 50% growth while quietly running -25% EBITDA margins — a Rule of 40 score of 15. He read "look how much revenue I own" on the slide; the buyer read "unit economics are not locked in" and priced him at 5x instead of 7x. When he tightened operations to -5% margin while holding 40% growth (score 35), his multiple recovered to 6.5x–7.5x. The story is not ours — it is from the [RaiseReady exit playbook](https://www.raisereadybook.com/blog/saas-exit-multiples-what-drives-valuation-for-recurring-revenue-businesses.html) — but we have seen the same arc play out locally, and the lesson travels: **growth without a credible path to margin is a discount, not a premium.**

Practical note for early-stage founders: if you are pre-profit by design (you are deliberately reinvesting), buyers apply the Rule of 40 with a twist — they want the score *approaching* 38–40 at exit, with evidence the margin side is real. A -40% margin with a story is not a plan. Show the cohort economics.

## The AI premium is real — but narrow

Here is where it gets interesting, because the market is not discounting everything equally. It is running a two-tier system.

AI-native software companies — products where AI is the core value driver, not a feature — achieved a **median 28x revenue multiple at secondary events versus 15x for traditional SaaS**, roughly a 93% premium. Even Gong, which saw its valuation compress from $7.2B to $4.5B, still achieved about **15x on $300M ARR** in its secondary round — nearly four times the overall private SaaS median. The market is paying up for AI that demonstrably creates revenue.

But — and this is the part founders keep misreading — the premium applies to *measurable AI impact*. StartuPage's 2026 guide puts it precisely: AI companies get a 26–42% premium, "but only with measurable AI impact." Our studio's working rule: if removing your AI features would not change your retention or your pricing power, you have no AI premium. You have a chatbot.

Worse, AI can actively hurt your multiple. Every token burned is a cost, and compute-heavy products with per-seat pricing have an ugly unit-economics story: your heaviest users are your least profitable accounts. We wrote recently about [AI automation ROI and the costs vendors never mention](/blog/ai-automation-roi-2026), and the same logic applies at the valuation table. A buyer diligence-ing your product will find your token bills. If your gross margin has sagged from 85% to 62% because of inference costs and you have no usage-based pricing to recover them, that shows up as margin compression — which feeds straight into your Rule of 40, which feeds straight into your multiple.

The winning move is boring: meter your AI costs, price them into your tiers, and prove the AI feature lifts retention or expansion. Then it is an asset. Otherwise it is a liability wearing a premium costume.

## The six numbers that move your multiple

When buyers argue about your price, they are arguing about six metrics. Fix these and the multiple follows; ignore them and no amount of narrative saves you.

| Metric | What buyers want | Why it moves the multiple |
|---|---|---|
| **ARR growth** | 30%+ (40%+ for premium) | The single biggest lever; 40%+ growth pushes you toward 7–10x |
| **Net revenue retention** | 110%+ (120%+ is elite) | NRR above 120% nearly doubles your multiple vs the 100–110% band |
| **Gross revenue retention** | 90%+ (95%+ world-class) | Proves product-market fit and kills churn-discount arguments |
| **Gross margin** | 75–90% | Shows the business scales without proportional cost increases |
| **Rule of 40 score** | ≥ 40 | Strongest single predictor; each point short is a discount |
| **Customer concentration** | No account > 10–15% of ARR | Removes key-customer risk that buyers price in harshly |

Two of these deserve extra attention because founders consistently underestimate them.

**Net revenue retention is the closest thing to a cheat code.** Same growth rate, same revenue, different NRR — that is a seven-figure difference in exit value, as the RaiseReady analysis demonstrates with a worked $20M example. Expansion revenue from existing customers is the cheapest growth in the business: no CAC, no sales cycle, and it proves the product is becoming more valuable over time. If your NRR is sitting at 102% and you are spending all your energy on new logos, you are misallocating effort. One customer success hire focused on expansion can be worth more than three AEs.

**Gross margin tells the efficiency story.** World-class SaaS runs 75–90%. Below 70%, buyers start asking whether the business is really software or a services company with a login page — and services companies do not get SaaS multiples. If you are Pakistan-based with a US revenue base, you have a genuine structural advantage here: engineering and support costs in PKR against ARR priced in USD. We have seen lean teams hold 85%+ gross margins that US-staffed competitors cannot touch. That is not a footnote; in a market that prizes profitable growth, it is a valuation weapon. More on that below.

## Exits in 2026: M&A, IPO or secondary?

The exit landscape shapes what "value" even means for you, so let us map it.

**M&A is where the action is.** Global SaaS M&A hit roughly 2,700 transactions in 2025, up 28% year over year, and H1 2026 pushed value to a decade high — but concentrated in AI-driven strategic acquisitions. Strategic buyers (the incumbents buying AI capability or market share) pay the highest multiples because they buy synergies: your technology fills their gap, your customers expand their platform, your team brings expertise they lack. PE and growth-equity firms pay lower multiples because they are buying your P&L. Know which buyer you are building for, because it changes everything from your tech stack to your customer mix.

![An abstract illustration of a founder deal: two geometric hands exchanging a glowing ring over dark contractual glass panels](/blog/saas-valuation-multiples-2026/fig2.webp)

**The IPO window is narrow and unforgiving.** Twenty-three SaaS IPOs in H1 2026, mixed post-listing performance, and public investors who punish misses instantly. Unless you are at serious scale with clean profitability, an IPO is not your exit plan in 2026 — it is a lottery ticket.

**Secondaries are the quiet release valve.** With exits slow and PE fundraising on pace for a third straight annual decline (dry powder stagnant near $4.4 trillion, net cash flows negative since 2022), more founders and early employees are taking liquidity through secondary transactions instead of waiting for a full exit. If you need personal liquidity but believe in the company's trajectory, a structured secondary at a fair multiple beats a fire-sale acquisition every time.

Our honest read: for most founders reading this, the realistic 2026–2027 outcomes are a strategic acquisition or a PE-backed growth recap. Both reward the same thing — clean metrics, profitable growth, defensible product. Build for that and you keep every option open.

## What this means if you are building from Pakistan

This section is for the founders in Lahore, Karachi and Islamabad selling into the US and Europe, because the global data lands differently on you — mostly in your favour, if you play it right.

**Your cost structure is a multiple-expander.** A US-staffed SaaS company at $5M ARR might run 40–50% EBITDA margins after a $150k-per-engineer payroll. The same product built with a senior-heavy Pakistan team can run equivalent output at a fraction of the cost, which shows up directly in your Rule of 40 score. In a market that pays premiums for profitable growth, lean is not a compromise — it is the strategy. We have built and advised products where the margin story alone justified an extra turn of multiple, because the buyer could see the profit was structural, not engineered for the sale.

**Your revenue story needs USD proof.** Buyers discount ARR they cannot verify. Price in USD, bill through a recognised processor (Stripe Atlas, Paddle, or a US entity), and keep revenue recognition immaculate. Nothing kills a multiple faster than a buyer discovering your "$2M ARR" mixes annual prepayments, one-off services and a founder's optimistic spreadsheet. Clean books are a valuation lever — treat them like product.

**Do not price yourself like an outsourcing shop.** This is the mistake we see most. Founders with world-class product quality apologise for their location by discounting — and then wonder why their valuation trails. Buyers do not pay for your cost base; they pay for your metrics. A 120% NRR is a 120% NRR whether the team sits in San Francisco or Gulberg. Price like a software company, because you are one.

**Mind the exit geography.** Most of your realistic acquirers are US or European strategics. That means Delaware or UK holding structures, clean IP assignment, and contracts governed by law your buyer recognises. Sorting this out during diligence costs you leverage; sorting it out now costs you a few thousand dollars and a week.

## A 90-day plan to raise your multiple

You cannot change the market. You can change your position in it. Here is what we would do if a founder walked into our studio today with a raise or an exit conversation 90 days out.

**Days 1–30: get the numbers honest.** Reconcile ARR properly — no services revenue, no optimistic annualisations. Compute your real Rule of 40, NRR, GRR and gross margin. Identify your five largest accounts and their share of ARR. Most founders find one skeleton in this exercise: the churned-but-still-counted customer, the "expansion" that was actually a discount reversal. Fix the definitions now, because a buyer's diligence will fix them later at your expense.

**Days 31–60: attack the two highest-leverage metrics.** For most companies that is NRR and gross margin. On NRR: instrument expansion — usage-based upsells, seat expansion triggers, annual-plan migrations with a real incentive. On margin: audit your AI and infrastructure costs per account (if one enterprise customer is eating 30% of your token budget, that is a pricing problem, not a tech problem) and renegotiate or re-tier. We covered the [SaaS benchmarks investors actually check](/blog/saas-benchmarks-2026-founders) in detail — use them as your dashboard.

**Days 61–90: package the story.** Build the one-page metrics sheet a buyer wants: ARR, growth, NRR, GRR, gross margin, Rule of 40, logo churn, CAC payback. Write the narrative honestly — where you are strong, where you are improving, what the next owner gets. Then pressure-test it with someone who has sat on the buy side. The founders who get premium multiples are not the ones with perfect numbers; they are the ones whose numbers survive scrutiny.

One thing not to do: manufacture a hockey-stick forecast to justify a 2021 multiple. Buyers have seen that slide ten thousand times. A credible plan at a fair multiple closes; a fantasy at a premium multiple dies in diligence. And if you are early — pre-revenue or under $1M ARR — none of this is a reason to panic. Multiples barely apply to you yet. Your job is to build the retention and margin habits now that become the metrics buyers price later.

## The bottom line

The 11.7x headline is not the market being unfair — it is the market being honest. SaaS businesses are being priced like businesses again: on cash flow, on retention, on real defensibility. The founders who thrive in this environment are the ones who stopped performing growth and started building companies.

Your multiple is not a lottery number. It is six metrics, a clean set of books, and a product whose value survives contact with an AI agent. Work the metrics, protect the margin, and the market will pay you — maybe not 2021 money, but real money, for a real business.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the average SaaS valuation multiple in 2026?

The median SaaS M&A deal priced at about 4.0x enterprise value to trailing-twelve-month revenue in Q2 2026, with founder-led lower-middle-market companies clearing roughly 3x to 6.5x ARR. Profitable mid-market SaaS businesses are typically valued on EBITDA at 10x to 18x. AI-native products with measurable AI revenue can command a steep premium — but ordinary SaaS no longer carries one.

### How is a SaaS company valued?

Mostly on a multiple of annual recurring revenue (ARR), adjusted for growth rate, net revenue retention, gross margin, churn and customer concentration. Profitable, mature companies are cross-checked or fully valued on an EBITDA multiple. Buyers then sanity-check both against comparable transactions and a discounted cash flow model, and the negotiation usually comes down to which method governs.

### What is the Rule of 40 and why do buyers care?

The Rule of 40 says your revenue growth rate plus your EBITDA margin should sum to at least 40. It is the market's shorthand for efficient scaling: growth without profitability is expensive, and profit without growth is stagnant. Companies clearing 40 consistently price one to two turns of ARR above otherwise comparable peers — it is arguably the strongest single predictor of your multiple.

### Why did SaaS valuation multiples fall in 2026?

Three forces converged: structurally higher interest rates repriced every long-duration cash flow, AI disruption risk made buyers discount software that could be unbundled by an agent, and a shift in investor taste from growth-at-all-costs to profitable growth. Median SaaS EV/EBITDA for PE deals fell to 11.7x from 20.4x, nearly converging with non-SaaS multiples.

### When is the best time to sell a SaaS business?

When the metrics are at their strongest: growing above 30%, net revenue retention above 110%, gross margins above 80%, and Rule of 40 at or above 40. Roughly 2,700 SaaS M&A transactions closed in 2025, so buyer demand exists — but in 2026 buyers pay premiums only for clean, verifiable numbers. Waiting for a "better market" while metrics soften is how founders sell low.

### Do AI features increase a SaaS company's valuation?

Only when AI is a core value driver with measurable revenue impact, not a bolted-on chatbot. AI-native software companies achieved roughly double the revenue multiples of traditional SaaS in recent secondary transactions. Slapping a wrapper around an LLM while margins bleed compute costs can actually hurt your multiple, because buyers see unprofitable unit economics.

### How can a founder raise their SaaS valuation multiple?

The levers are well documented: grow ARR above 30%+, lift net revenue retention above 110–120%, hold gross margins above 75–80%, hit a Rule of 40 score of 40+, reduce customer concentration, and clean up financials and revenue recognition so a buyer's diligence finds no surprises. NRR above 120% nearly doubles your multiple versus the 100–110% band.
