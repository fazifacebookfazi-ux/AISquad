### What EliseAI actually sells

Here is the part founders miss. EliseAI does not sell "AI for property management." It sells fewer vacant units, faster lease-ups, maintenance requests that don't fall through the cracks. The customer buys an outcome — occupied apartments — and the software happens to be how it's delivered.

That is the vertical SaaS contract: a horizontal tool sells features, a vertical tool sells the job done in the customer's own language. When budgets tighten, features get cut. The job still needs doing.

## Vertical SaaS vs horizontal SaaS: the honest comparison

Founders routinely confuse "niche marketing" with a vertical product. The difference:

| | Horizontal SaaS | Vertical SaaS |
|---|---|---|
| **Sells to** | Every industry, one function | One industry, every function |
| **Examples** | Slack, Salesforce, Notion | ServiceTitan, Toast, Veeva, EliseAI |
| **Go-to-market** | Broad marketing, self-serve, PLG | Industry channels, partnerships, field sales |
| **Onboarding** | Minutes to hours | Weeks to months (data migration, compliance) |
| **Churn** | Higher; easy to rip out | Lower; embedded in operations |
| **Pricing power** | Weak; compared against dozens of tools | Strong; priced against the cost of the problem |
| **Second revenue stream** | Rare | Embedded fintech (payments, lending, insurance) |
| **AI moat** | Thin — features are copyable | Deep — proprietary workflow data |

Different games, different physics. Horizontal is land-grab: win on network effects, brand and distribution; lose slowly to a thousand competitors. Vertical is trench warfare: win by knowing the industry's regulations, busy seasons, paperwork and unspoken rules better than anyone; lose only to a deeper specialist.

The honest trade-off for a founder: vertical SaaS has a smaller addressable market, slower sales cycles, and onboarding that will test your patience. In exchange, you get customers who almost never leave, pricing power that horizontal founders envy, and a payments business hiding inside your software business.

### Why generic tools keep losing the regulated, workflow-heavy industries

A generic CRM doesn't know that a construction bid in one county requires a compliance document that the neighbouring county doesn't. A generic scheduling tool doesn't know the difference between a dental emergency slot and a routine cleaning, or that a misfiled insurance claim costs a clinic real money. In industries where the workflow *is* the product — healthcare, legal, construction, logistics, home services — the generic tool is a tax on the user's time, and users eventually refuse to pay it.

This is why [September's B2B SaaS trend data](https://blog.mean.ceo/b2b-saas-trends-september-2026/) keeps repeating the same line: industry-specific products beat generic tools when workflows, vocabulary and regulation matter. Agents don't use software — they execute processes, and a process the agent doesn't understand is one it can't automate.

## Why the money is moving here: five forces

The funding charts are a symptom. Here is the disease — five structural forces, all pushing in the same direction, all accelerating in 2026.

### Force 1: AI agents compress team size

AI agents are collapsing the cost of building and running software. Solo founders run serious products for under $500/month in infrastructure, and startups hit $5–10M ARR with teams of fewer than five people. The barrier to *entry* has never been lower — and the barrier to *being noticed* has never been higher.

A horizontal product built by three people competes against ten thousand other three-person teams. A vertical product built by three people with five years in the industry competes against almost nobody. When software is cheap to build, industry knowledge is the scarce input.

### Force 2: Seat pricing is collapsing

This is the big one. Corporate clients are cutting seat licences as agents automate workflows — the "seat compression effect" — and vendors are scrambling toward usage- and outcome-based pricing. Gartner forecasts that by the end of 2026, over 80% of software providers incorporating AI will be forced to abandon traditional per-seat pricing. [Analysis in the Jerusalem Post](https://www.jpost.com/business-and-innovation/article-908502) puts the sharper point: AI didn't kill SaaS margins, it exposed broken revenue infrastructure. McKinsey's numbers show companies that fail to adapt their financial plumbing to dynamic models suffering double-digit erosion in gross margins.

Vertical SaaS is structurally insulated. When you sell to plumbers, you were never selling seats — you were selling booked jobs. The vertical product's pricing was always closer to the outcome than the login, so the seat-compression crisis mostly compresses someone else's business model.

### Force 3: Embedded fintech is the real margin

Now the number every founder should tattoo on their forearm. The most successful vertical SaaS companies don't earn their margin on subscriptions. They earn it on the money moving through their customers' businesses:

| Company | Vertical | The split |
|---|---|---|
| Toast | Restaurants | $5B financial services vs $936M software subscriptions |
| Shopify | Commerce | 73% of revenue from merchant solutions, not subscriptions |
| ServiceTitan | Home services | 70% subscription / 25% fintech / 5% services |
| EliseAI | Housing + healthcare | Software entry point; AI teammate sold on operational outcomes |

Toast is not a point-of-sale company that happens to do payments. It is a payments company wearing a point-of-sale costume — the software is the distribution channel for the fintech. This is the move horizontal SaaS almost never gets to make, because horizontal tools don't sit on top of an industry's money flows. Vertical tools do. If your vertical involves invoices, bookings, payouts or financing — and most do — your pricing strategy has a second act your competitors can't copy without your customer base.

### Force 4: Retention economics favour the specialist

Churn destroys SaaS businesses faster than weak lead generation, and vertical products churn at a fraction of horizontal rates. ServiceTitan holds gross retention above 95% with average revenue per customer around $78,000 a year. The median B2B SaaS net revenue retention sits around 106%; enterprise segments reach 115–125% through expansion, and vertical products with embedded payments expand almost automatically as their customers' transaction volume grows.

Why? Ripping out a vertical product means re-migrating years of operational data, retraining the staff, and breaking the payments integration — weeks of business disruption, not an afternoon of CSV exports. Nobody churns from software that runs their payroll, dispatch and money.

### Force 5: A $450B market is being rebuilt, not destroyed

a16z puts the vertical SaaS market at roughly $450B and estimates 30–40% of it will be reshaped by AI agents between 2026 and 2028. Reshaped — not erased. IDC pegs industry-specific AI solutions growing at 36.5% CAGR against 18.9% for general AI. BCG finds 70% of the highest-ROI AI deployments embed inside existing business processes rather than replacing them.

The money agrees: [Forvis Mazars' H1 2026 State of SaaS](https://www.forvismazars.us/forsights/2026/09/h1-2026-state-of-saas) reports 81.7% of SaaS M&A value tied to AI-related transactions ($439.7B deployed) — but median private-equity multiples compressed to 11.7x EBITDA from 20.4x in 2025. Capital still flows, but only to real differentiation. "AI-powered" is no longer the differentiator. "Owns the workflow of one industry" is.

## Picking your vertical: the niching playbook

This is where most founders stall. They know vertical is the play, then stare at a blank page trying to pick *which* vertical. Here is the framework we use with clients.

![Abstract illustration of concentric glowing rings narrowing focus onto a single point of light, representing choosing a vertical SaaS niche](/blog/vertical-saas-founder-playbook-2026/fig2.webp)

### The three-question test

Run every candidate industry through these three questions. If it fails any one of them, walk away.

**1. Is the pain frequent and expensive?** You want problems that recur weekly, not annually, and cost real money each time they go wrong. Missed appointments, compliance fines, spoiled inventory, idle crews. Annual tax filing is a bad vertical; daily dispatch is a great one.

**2. Does the buyer have budget authority?** The person who feels the pain must be able to sign the cheque. Software sold to employees with no purchasing power dies in "let me check with my manager" purgatory. Owner-operators, practice managers, dispatchers with P&L responsibility — these are your buyers.

**3. Is there a data moat to build?** Ask what proprietary data accumulates as customers use the product: job histories, pricing patterns, compliance outcomes, customer behaviour. If the answer is "nothing," the vertical is defendable only by execution, and execution is copyable. The [vertical SaaS 2.0 thesis](https://editorialge.com/saas-trends-q1/) is explicit: the moat is no longer the software, it's the proprietary data used to train the vertical agents.

### Niches working right now

You don't need our permission to pick these, but the pattern is instructive — every one of them pairs a workflow-heavy industry with an underserved buyer:

- **Housing and healthcare operations** — EliseAI's $4B verdict. Admin-heavy, regulated, desperate for reliable automation.
- **Independent restaurants** — Owner's $240M Series E. Thin margins, high turnover, allergic to generic tools.
- **Legal** — DeepJudge's $41.2M Series A; Harvey at 80% adoption in 45 days. A 70% cut in research time is a hiring decision avoided.
- **Procurement** — Omnea's $50M Series B. Agentic buying is becoming a real purchasing channel.
- **Home services** — ServiceTitan's kingdom: $78k ARPA, 95%+ gross retention.
- **Blue-collar industries** — construction, logistics, manufacturing, warehousing. Logibot and Factrika are this month's evidence.
- **Regulated paperwork** — Charikaty doing company formation across Morocco and the Gulf. Boring filings, real fees.

Notice what they share: the founders (or their teams) understand the industry's paperwork better than the industry's incumbents understand software. That is the whole hiring criterion. If you can't name the three most annoying forms in your target industry from memory, you are not ready to build for it — go work in it, or partner with someone who has.

## From zero to first ten customers

Vertical SaaS sales don't look like horizontal SaaS sales. There is no viral loop, no Product Hunt launch that matters, no self-serve funnel printing customers while you sleep — at least not at first. Here's how the first ten actually happen.

### Validate in the industry's own language

Forget landing pages with "AI-powered solution" headlines. The validation conversation sounds like this: you sit with an owner-operator, describe their Tuesday in their own vocabulary — the dispatch board, the no-show slots, the invoice chase — and watch their face. If they lean in and start telling *you* how it should work, you have something. If they nod politely, you don't.

The founders who make it do something unfashionable: four to eight weeks inside the industry before writing code. Shadow the dispatcher. Sit in the clinic. Ride along with the crew. You learn which 10% of the workflow causes 90% of the pain — the only part worth building first.

### Build the workflow, not the platform

The classic vertical SaaS mistake is building a platform: dashboards, settings pages, role management, integrations — a beautiful empty shell. The customer doesn't want a platform. They want Tuesday to go smoothly.

Build one workflow end to end — booking → reminders → check-in → billing for a clinic; order → route → proof of delivery → invoicing for logistics. Make that single thread genuinely excellent, charge for it, then widen. Toast started as point-of-sale software restaurants liked; the fintech empire came later, riding on that customer base.

On cost: [our 2026 breakdown of SaaS MVP pricing](https://www.aisquadx.tech/blog/saas-mvp-cost-2026) covers the mechanics, but the vertical-specific version is this. In our studio's observed range, a validation prototype — one workflow, ugly but functional — runs PKR 250k–600k ($900–$2,100). A real MVP that a paying customer can run their business on runs PKR 1.2M–3.5M ($4,300–$12,500) over 10–16 weeks. A full vertical build with integrations, compliance handling and embedded payments runs PKR 4M–9M ($14k–$32k). The code is the cheap part. The industry research, the data-migration tooling and the compliance layer are where the money goes — budget for them honestly or they will ambush you in month four.

### The data migration is the onboarding

Here is the vertical SaaS truth nobody tells horizontal founders: there is no freemium. There is no trial. There is the moment the customer cuts over from the old system to yours — go-live — and everything before it is preparation, everything after it is operation.

The data import is not a step in the onboarding; it *is* the moat. ServiceTitan dedicates roughly four weeks of a 12–16 week implementation to data discovery and migration. Toast runs 4–8 weeks. The customer who has migrated five years of operational data onto your platform is essentially never leaving — and that stickiness is the feature you're really selling, even if the brochure talks about the dashboard.

Plan for it from day one. Build migration tooling before you need it, price the implementation separately or bake it into annual contracts — but never pretend it doesn't exist. Founders who treat onboarding as a cost centre churn; those who treat it as the moat retain.

## Pricing: charge for the outcome, collect on the rails

Vertical SaaS pricing has two layers, and most founders only build the first.

**Layer one: the subscription as entry ticket.** Price it to cover costs and filter for serious customers. Flat per-location or per-team pricing beats per-seat — your customers think in branches, crews and chairs, not logins. Price per chair, per location, per vehicle: match how the customer counts their own business.

**Layer two: the outcome and the rails.** This is where 2026 is heading. Ema prices on completed workflows, not seats — and posted 180% net dollar retention doing it. When your software books the appointment, processes the payment or files the compliance report, charging a slice of the completed transaction aligns your revenue with your customer's success in a way seat pricing never can.

Then comes the rails. The moment your product touches money — and in most verticals it will — embed payments, lending or insurance and take your basis points. Toast's $5B in financial services revenue is the proof; Shopify's 73% merchant-solutions share is the corroboration. For founders serving Pakistan, the rails look different — bank transfers, JazzCash/Easypaisa integrations, reconciliation tooling — but the principle is identical: sit on the money flow and the margin follows. Even a 1–2% take rate will, within two years, dwarf the subscription revenue.

One caution: don't lead with outcome pricing on day one. You need six to twelve months of usage data before you know what an outcome is actually worth to customers and what it costs you to deliver — especially when AI inference is part of the cost stack. Start with the subscription, instrument everything, then move your best customers to outcome pricing once the numbers are undeniable. The migration is the product strategy.

## Retention is the growth engine

September's trend data keeps repeating it: retention has become the growth engine, and churn kills faster than weak lead gen. In vertical SaaS, retention is a design discipline with three parts.

### The champion strategy

Inside every customer organisation, one person becomes your platform's internal champion — the dispatcher who masters the system, the practice manager who lives in it, the partner who runs their matters through it. Your onboarding's secret job is to *create* this person. Vendors that supply structured onboarding and identify the champion in week one see adoption like Harvey's 80% in 45 days; without a champion, rollouts stall and the renewal conversation gets awkward.

So: name the champion in the sales process, train them first, give them a direct line to your team, and make them look good internally. Champions don't churn, and they bring you the next three locations when the business expands.

### The numbers you actually watch

Forget vanity metrics. The vertical SaaS dashboard that matters:

| Metric | Healthy range | Why it matters for vertical |
|---|---|---|
| Gross revenue retention | 90%+ (best-in-class 95%+) | Measures the stickiness of the workflow itself |
| Net revenue retention | 106%+ (enterprise 115–125%) | Expansion via seats, locations, transaction volume |
| Time to first value | Under 30 days post go-live | Slow value = stalled rollout = churn risk |
| Feature depth | 3+ core features adopted | Users on 3+ features churn at roughly half the rate of single-feature users |
| Champion health | Named, trained, active weekly | No champion, no renewal — track it like revenue |

Two traps: don't benchmark NRR against horizontal giants — your gross retention does the heavy lifting — and track churn by *cohort and cause*, not as a blended number. Losing price-sensitive single-location accounts while multi-location ones expand is a healthy business wearing a scary headline.

## What goes wrong: the three failure modes

We have watched enough vertical SaaS attempts — ours and our clients' — to name the ways they die. Three patterns cover nearly all of them.

### Too narrow, too early

"Dental appointment reminders for orthodontists in Lahore" is not a business; it's a feature with rent to pay. The vertical has to be narrow enough to dominate but wide enough to support ten, then a hundred, then a thousand paying customers. The test: can you name 500 potential customers from memory or a single afternoon of research? If not, widen the vertical — "dental clinics" instead of "orthodontists," "home services" instead of "HVAC" — until the maths works, then narrow your *messaging* within it.

### Generalising too soon

More common: the product works for plumbers, so the founder adds electricians, then roofers, then "all field services" — and within a year it's a generic scheduling tool with none of the depth plumbers loved. Every new vertical resets your moat to zero. Add a second only when the first runs without you.

### Skipping the compliance layer

This one kills quietly. You build for clinics without patient-data rules, for lenders without audit trails — and the product works beautifully until the first enterprise security review or regulator's letter. With procurement teams asking about data lineage early in 2026, compliance is table stakes, not a Series A feature. Budget 15–20% of build cost for it in regulated verticals.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is vertical SaaS?

Vertical SaaS is software built for one specific industry — dentists, property managers, HVAC companies — instead of one function across all industries. It speaks the industry's vocabulary, handles its compliance, and automates its exact workflows. ServiceTitan (home services), Toast (restaurants), Veeva (life sciences) and EliseAI (housing and healthcare) are the canonical examples. Horizontal products win on breadth and network effects; vertical products win on depth — they know the regulations, the paperwork, the seasonality. In 2026, vertical products are winning the retention game because AI agents make deep workflow automation more valuable than another generic dashboard.
