# Headless CMS in 2026: Sanity vs Strapi vs Payload — Our Studio Verdict

Your developer says the new site needs a "headless CMS." Your marketing lead hears that and assumes it means more expensive, more complicated, and probably a worse editing experience. You are the one with the budget, and both of them are pointing at you.

Here is the short version before we earn the long one: a headless CMS splits your website's content from its design. Content lives in one system; your developers build the visible site on top of it with an API. That decoupling is genuinely powerful for the right project — and genuinely wasteful for the wrong one. We have built both kinds. The difference between a smart headless build and an expensive mistake is not the tool you pick. It is knowing whether you needed headless at all.

In this guide we compare the four platforms that win real projects in 2026 — **Sanity, Strapi, Payload CMS and Contentful** — plus the middle path of headless WordPress. Real pricing in USD and PKR. Real trade-offs from production projects. And a verdict, because that is what you are here for.

## What "headless" actually means (and why most people misunderstand it)

A traditional CMS like WordPress is two things at once: the place you edit content, and the thing that draws the page your visitor sees. A headless CMS keeps the first job and fires itself from the second. The content becomes structured data — headlines, paragraphs, images, products — served through an API. Anything can read that API: a Next.js website, a mobile app, a voice assistant, an AI agent.

That is the whole idea. Everything else is marketing.

![Headless architecture: structured content blocks flowing into multiple devices](/blog/headless-cms-guide-2026/fig1.webp)

### When headless earns its keep

Headless pays off in four situations, and in our experience almost only these four:

1. **You publish across channels.** Website, mobile app, maybe a kiosk or a customer portal — all reading the same content. Write once, deliver everywhere.
2. **Your frontend is custom.** A marketing site with heavy motion, a configurator, a dashboard. Anything a theme cannot express without being beaten into submission.
3. **Your content team is real.** Multiple editors, review workflows, scheduled publishing. Headless admin UIs are usually calmer than the WordPress dashboard.
4. **AI systems need to read you.** [Discovery is splitting into two paths: classic search and AI-generated answers](https://megatechbot.com/blog/website-development-trends-for-haryana-businesses-in-2026). Clean, structured, API-served content is trivially readable by both humans and models.

### When it is overkill

A 10-page brochure site with a contact form does not need a headless architecture. We turn this work down — politely — because the bill is real: a proper headless build needs actual frontend development, not a theme install. You will pay for two systems (CMS + frontend) and two hosting setups. If your site changes four times a year, a solid WordPress build or even a static site is faster, cheaper, and easier to hand over.

The honest rule we give clients: **if your editing surface is a page and your output is a page, stay traditional.** If your editing surface is structured data and your outputs are plural, go headless.

## Sanity: the polished managed option

Sanity is the headless CMS you choose when you never want to think about servers. It is fully managed SaaS: the Content Lake, the CDN, the image pipeline, all of it lives on Sanity's infrastructure. Your editors work in the Sanity Studio, your developers query content with GROQ (Sanity's query language) or GraphQL.

### What we like

The editing experience is the best in the category. Real-time collaboration genuinely works — two editors in the same document without conflicts, live previews that do not lag. Structured content is treated as a first-class concept, not a blog-post-with-extra-fields. And because everything is hosted, there is no DevOps story to manage: no Docker, no database tuning, no 2 a.m. pager duty because Postgres ran out of disk.

### What costs you

Sanity's Growth plan is roughly **$15 per editor per month** once you exceed the free tier's 3 users, and usage add-ons stack on top: bandwidth, API CDN requests, datasets. At five editors you are near $75/month plus usage; at twenty editors you are at $300/month before a single page loads. It is not extortionate, but it is the opposite of cost-certain. The free tier (3 users, 10 GB bandwidth, 1 M CDN requests) is genuinely generous for small projects, which is why we use it for MVPs.

There is also lock-in of a quieter kind: your content lives in Sanity's Content Lake, queried in Sanity's GROQ. Migrating out means rewriting queries, not just exporting JSON.

### The verdict on Sanity

Pick Sanity when the team editing the site is non-technical or distributed, when nobody on your side wants to run infrastructure, and when per-seat pricing will not embarrass you at scale. It is the calmest option. It is also the one whose bill grows silently — audit it every quarter.

## Strapi: the self-hosted workhorse

Strapi is open-source and self-hosted by default. You run it on a VPS, Railway, Render, your own cluster — wherever you like. The Community edition is free forever, and Strapi Cloud exists if you want the managed route (Cloud Pro at roughly **$99/month**).

### What we like

Control. Full stop. Your data lives where you put it — a client with data-residency requirements, or a Pakistani business that wants its customer data in a region it trusts, gets exactly that. No per-seat pricing means a fifty-editor newsroom costs the same in licences as a two-person shop: **zero**. The plugin marketplace is mature, the admin UI is the easiest for non-developers to learn, and the REST/GraphQL APIs are conventional enough that any backend developer is productive in a day.

For Pakistan-based businesses, there is a second, quieter advantage: a self-hosted Strapi on a cheap VPS can cost **$5–20/month total** for a small project. At a time when the rupee makes every dollar subscription hurt, that matters.

### What costs you

You are the infrastructure team now. Updates, backups, security patches, database sizing — yours. A Strapi server nobody patches is a liability, and we have inherited projects where that is exactly what happened. The managed Cloud tier fixes this but then you are paying SaaS money anyway, just with fairer terms than the seat-priced competition.

Strapi also shows its age in the content-modelling UI: it is visual and friendly, but complex relational content models get fiddly. For very content-rich sites, Sanity's code-defined schemas scale better.

### The verdict on Strapi

Pick Strapi when you have (or will hire) someone who can keep a server alive, when data location or licence cost at scale matters, or when you are building for the Pakistani market where self-hosting keeps the rupee bill down. We recommend it more often than any other option, mostly because the failure mode — an unpatched server — is preventable, while the failure modes of the SaaS options are bills.

## Payload CMS: the developer's favourite

Payload is the new-school option: an open-source (MIT) CMS that ships as a Node package running *inside your own project*. There is no separate CMS server — Payload **is** your backend. It connects to MongoDB or Postgres, exposes REST and GraphQL, and generates its own admin UI from your code-defined schema. The big 2026 change: Postgres support matured properly in v3, which removed the last serious reason many teams hesitated.

### What we like

Payload is the best CMS we have used inside a Next.js project, full stop. TypeScript end to end: your content types are your actual types. Define a schema in code, get a validated admin UI for free, get type-safe API access in the same repo. No context-switching between a CMS dashboard and your codebase. For teams already on Next.js — which, per our [framework guide](/blog/best-web-development-framework-2026), is our studio pick for most business sites in 2026 — Payload feels like it was designed to sit next to you.

Local development needs zero external services. Spin it up, model content, ship. Payload Cloud exists for managed hosting (free tier plus paid plans), but self-hosting stays free forever, on infrastructure as cheap as Strapi's.

### What costs you

The admin UI, while auto-generated and clean, is less forgiving for large non-technical editing teams than Strapi's or Sanity's. Your editors will feel that the schema was designed by developers — because it was. The ecosystem is smaller than Strapi's: fewer plugins, fewer Stack Overflow answers, fewer agencies that know it. And because Payload lives in your codebase, your developers own it the way they own everything else; there is no vendor to blame when the admin panel breaks.

### The verdict on Payload

Pick Payload when your team is TypeScript-first, your project is Next.js, and you want content modelling to live in version control next to the code. It is the strongest technical choice of 2026 for full-stack teams — with the honest caveat that you need a developer in the room, always.

## Contentful: the enterprise incumbent (briefly)

Contentful deserves a paragraph, not a section, because in 2026 it is priced for a different customer. Basic starts at **$300/month** for 5 users and capped API calls; realistic mid-tier usage lands around **$2,000/month**; enterprise quotes run $60k–$140k/year. The platform is solid — REST and GraphQL, good webhooks, the original headless product — but the math stopped working for the mid-market.

Our position: if you are already on Contentful at enterprise scale with multi-locale publishing and SSO requirements, stay. The migration cost dwarfs the savings. If you are choosing fresh in 2026 and Contentful is on the shortlist, you should be comparing it against Sanity at a tenth the price and asking what the extra money buys you. Usually: procurement comfort and a longer feature list your editors will not use.

## The middle path: headless WordPress

Before you abandon WordPress, consider the compromise: keep WordPress as the editing backend, expose it headlessly via the REST API or WPGraphQL, and build the frontend in Next.js or Astro. Your editors keep the dashboard they know. Your visitors get a fast modern frontend. Your developers get off the theme treadmill.

We have shipped this for clients whose content teams would have revolted at a new admin UI. The trade-offs are real: the API is slower and less structured than a purpose-built headless CMS, plugin updates can break the API surface, and you still host WordPress (with its security-update obligations). But the retraining cost is zero, which in a ten-person marketing team is worth more than any benchmark.

One warning from practice: headless WordPress tempts teams to keep bolting plugins onto the backend "because it's still WordPress." Every plugin is a potential API surprise. Lock the backend down, disable what you do not need, and treat it as a content API with a familiar admin skin.

## How to choose: the decision framework we actually use

Forget the feature matrices. In discovery calls we ask four questions, and the answers pick the CMS:

**1. Who edits, and how technical are they?**
Non-technical marketing team, no developer on staff → Sanity (managed, calm UI) or Strapi Cloud (friendly UI, simpler bill). Developer-led team on TypeScript → Payload. Mixed team with strict data rules → self-hosted Strapi.

**2. Who keeps the servers alive?**
"Nobody" is a valid answer — it picks Sanity or a Cloud tier. If you have DevOps (or a studio on retainer — we do offer [ongoing support](/services)), self-hosting Strapi or Payload is the cheapest long-term option.

**3. How many editors, honestly?**
Three editors: anyone works, pick on ergonomics. Thirty editors: per-seat pricing is now the dominant cost — self-host Strapi or Payload and stop the bleed. This is the question that quietly disqualifies Sanity and Contentful for newsrooms and large content teams.

**4. What does the frontend need?**
Next.js project with custom everything → Payload (it lives in the same repo). Multi-channel (web + app + kiosk) → Sanity's structured content model shines. AI agents or automation consuming content → anything with a clean API, but code-defined schemas (Payload, Sanity) are easier to keep honest than click-defined ones.

Notice what is not on the list: which CMS is "best." The best CMS is the one your team can run for three years without hating it.

## What goes wrong in practice

We have inherited enough headless projects to have opinions about the failure modes. The tools are rarely the problem. The decisions are.

**Choosing headless for a static site.** The classic: a company pays for a decoupled architecture, then the marketing team asks why they cannot just edit a page like before. The answer is that they bought a system designed for structured content and are using it as a page editor. Expensive lesson. Match the architecture to the editing pattern.

**Ignoring the API bill.** Seat-priced and usage-priced SaaS is calm at launch and feral at scale. A site whose traffic doubles twice will blow through Sanity's free-tier API requests and Contentful's caps. Model your costs at 10x traffic before you commit, not after the invoice.

**Client-side rendering the whole frontend.** Headless gives you a fast API; it does not give you a fast website. We still see headless sites that render everything in the browser, ship 2 MB of JavaScript, and fail [Core Web Vitals](/blog/core-web-vitals-2026) worse than the WordPress site they replaced. Server-render your pages. That is the entire point of the architecture.

**Treating the CMS as the SEO strategy.** A headless CMS neither helps nor hurts your rankings by itself. What it does is remove excuses: clean semantic HTML, full meta control, schema markup, fast loads — all the things Google and AI answer engines reward. But someone still has to do the work. If nobody owns SEO after launch, the CMS choice is decoration.

**Letting editors drown in structured content.** Structured content is a discipline. Without content guidelines, editors create seventeen variations of "hero section" and the design system quietly dies. Whoever designs the schema should also write the one-page guide for editors. We include that in every build now, after learning the hard way.

## The migration question

"Can we move our existing WordPress site to headless without losing rankings?" Yes, and we do it regularly. The playbook:

1. **Keep every URL.** The frontend must serve the same URL structure, with 1:1 redirects for anything that changes. Rankings live at URLs; move the URLs and you move the rankings.
2. **Migrate content programmatically.** Export from WordPress (WPGraphQL or the REST API), transform into your new schema, import into the new CMS. Do not copy-paste 400 pages by hand.
3. **Rebuild meta and schema first, design second.** Title tags, descriptions, canonical tags, FAQPage and LocalBusiness JSON-LD — all of it in place before launch, not after.
4. **Launch mid-week, watch Search Console for two weeks.** Traffic dips of 5–10% for a week are normal; a sustained drop means something broke. Have a rollback plan.

A word on timing: migrations go smoothest when the content is frozen during the move — no editor publishing new posts while URLs are being remapped. Plan a short content freeze, usually a weekend, and tell the team in advance. It saves the frantic Monday-morning reconciliation we have all lived through at least once.

The most common reason migrations fail is not technical. It is launching the new design and the new CMS in the same week, so when traffic dips nobody knows which change caused it. Sequence them: migrate the CMS, stabilise, then redesign.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a headless CMS in simple terms?**
A headless CMS stores your content as structured data and delivers it through an API, without controlling how it looks. Your website or app fetches the content and renders it in whatever design you build. Content and presentation are completely decoupled.

**Which is the best headless CMS in 2026: Sanity, Strapi or Payload?**
There is no universal winner. Sanity is the best managed option for teams that do not want to run servers. Strapi wins on open-source self-hosting and data control. Payload is the strongest pick for TypeScript and Next.js teams that want the CMS to live inside their own codebase.

**Is a headless CMS more expensive than WordPress?**
Often, yes. Self-hosted Strapi or Payload can run nearly free on infrastructure, while managed options start around $15–$99/month and enterprise tiers reach $2,000+/month. The bigger cost is the build itself — headless projects need real frontend development, whereas WordPress leans on themes.

**Do I need a headless CMS for a small business website?**
Usually not. A 10-page marketing site gains almost nothing from a headless architecture and pays extra build cost for it. Headless makes sense when you publish frequently, push content to multiple channels, or need a frontend that WordPress themes cannot deliver.

**Can I migrate from WordPress to a headless CMS?**
Yes. The standard route is to keep WordPress as a headless backend or migrate content into Sanity, Strapi or Payload with export and import tooling. Keep your URL structure and redirects intact so you do not lose rankings during the move.

**How does a headless CMS affect SEO?**
It neither helps nor hurts SEO by itself. A properly built headless frontend loads fast, serves clean semantic HTML and supports full schema markup — all ranking factors. But a badly built headless site performs worse than a basic WordPress install. Someone still has to do the SEO work.

**Which headless CMS is cheapest to run at scale?**
Self-hosted Strapi or Payload — you pay only for your own infrastructure (roughly $5–$20/month for a small project), with no per-seat or API-call charges. Managed SaaS options charge per seat or usage, which compounds as your editor team and traffic grow.
