## What OpenAI Dots actually are

Forget the hype for a minute and look at the specification that OpenAI published. A dot is:

- **Powered by GPT-6 Astra**, OpenAI's current flagship model.
- **Given its own cloud computer** — a persistent environment where it can run, keep state, and continue work between your sessions rather than forgetting everything when the chat window closes.
- **Connected to 4,000+ apps** through OpenAI's plugin ecosystem, which means it can act inside your existing tools rather than living in a chat bubble off to the side.
- **Always reachable** — "your dot is always within reach, whether it is through ChatGPT, Slack or Teams," in OpenAI's words. You can type, or "hop on a voice call when you need to talk something out."
- **Adaptive** — it learns your preferences, how you think, and what good looks like the more you work with it.

The rollout began immediately for ChatGPT Pro and Business Premium users in eligible markets, with Enterprise admins able to enable a beta. A few caveats matter: for Pro users, the launch excludes the European Economic Area, Switzerland and the UK for now; Business Premium users get it in all supported ChatGPT regions. And the first dot is free — for the first month, usage doesn't even count toward plan allowances, after which OpenAI will publish usage terms per plan. Longer term, OpenAI says you will be able to add more dots and scale each one's speed or monthly work capacity.

Read that last part carefully, because it is the business model: **one free agent to get you hooked, usage-based pricing once the habit is formed, and a path to an entire workforce of dots.** That is a familiar playbook — and a sensible reason to start your pilot during the free window, when experimentation costs nothing and the lessons are yours to keep.

## What it can't be trusted with — yet

Here is where we take a position, because the industry marketing will not: **always-on agents are not ready to run your business unsupervised.** The same launch week that brought dots also brought the clearest evidence of why. As agents start coordinating multi-step tasks with minimal human intervention, their infrastructure costs spike unpredictably — Cribl launched an entire product, StreamAI, just to route prompts to the right model and enforce spending cutoffs, because surprise token bills are now a real category of business expense.

The failure modes we watch for in every agent deployment:

- **Permission sprawl.** A dot that can act across 4,000 apps needs boundaries, not blanket access. Give it read access widely, write access narrowly. An agent that can update your CRM records can also, with one misjudged instruction, update them wrongly at scale.
- **Hallucinated confidence.** Models are built to produce plausible output. In a chatbot that is a draft you correct; in an agent that sends emails and updates records, it is an action you may not see. Anything customer-facing or financial needs a human approval gate. This is not paranoia — it is the lesson from every production agent deployment in 2026.
- **Cost without ceiling.** A dot working "around the clock" is also spending around the clock. OpenAI's free month is a gift, but it is also a deadline: know your usage patterns before usage terms land. Route routine work to cheaper paths, and keep expensive model calls for judgment-heavy tasks.
- **No institutional context.** A brand-new dot knows nothing about your business on day one. It learns, but early weeks are the riskiest — it will make confident mistakes about things you thought were obvious. Supervise more, not less, at the start.

The broader trend reports agree with this posture. The September 2026 consensus across industry analyses is consistent: human-in-the-loop systems are beating full autonomy in serious business settings, and custom evaluation is replacing blind trust in benchmarks. Deloitte reports that 74% of organisations expect to use agentic AI at least moderately by 2027 — and 85% expect to customise agents for their own business. Adoption is running ahead of readiness, which is precisely why governance is the differentiator now.

## Our five-step pilot plan

This is the exact sequence we would run for a client starting a Dots pilot this week. It is designed to produce evidence, not enthusiasm.

### Step 1: Pick one workflow, not five

Choose a task that is frequent, annoying, and low-stakes. Our favourites: daily lead triage, meeting-prep briefs, a morning digest of yesterday's support tickets. The workflow should be easy to verify ("did the digest arrive and was it correct?") and cheap to fail at. If the dot gets it wrong, a human notices in seconds and nothing breaks. Write the workflow down in one paragraph — the dot's instructions are just that paragraph, sharpened.

### Step 2: Map the permissions before the connections

Before connecting anything, list what the dot needs to read and what it needs to write. Default to read-mostly: read the inbox, write only to a draft folder. Read the CRM, write only to a staging field. This takes an hour and prevents the entire class of mistakes that make executives regret agent pilots. Microsoft's own guidance for production agents says the same thing — the limit in 2026 is not model quality, it is reliability inside a real system with real permissions.

### Step 3: Run it in shadow mode for a week

Let the dot do the work, but have a human review every output before it goes anywhere real. This is the phase most teams skip and the phase that matters most. You are not testing whether the dot can do the task — you are calibrating your trust in it. Log every correction. If you are correcting the same thing twice, fix the instruction. If you are correcting new things every day, the workflow is too ambiguous and you picked the wrong one.

### Step 4: Put a human gate on the consequential step

After the shadow week, keep one approval gate: the dot drafts, a human sends. The dot proposes, a human approves. This gate can shrink over time as evidence accumulates, but it should exist from day one and never disappear entirely for anything that touches money, customers, or public statements. In our experience, the gate also makes staff *more* willing to use the agent — people trust tools they can overrule.

### Step 5: Measure hours, not vibes

At the end of week three, count: hours of human time saved per week, error rate on reviewed outputs, and token cost per completed workflow. If the hours saved beat the cost by a comfortable margin, expand to a second workflow. If they don't, you have learned something cheap — which is the whole point of the free window. Persistent agents are worth building when the maths works; our guide to [persistent AI agents](/blog/persistent-ai-agents-business-2026) goes deeper on the always-on patterns that survive this test.
