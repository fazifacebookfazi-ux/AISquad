That last point matters. Spam updates enforce Google's existing published spam policies; they do not invent new rules. One thing to notice: Google has become noticeably quieter about targets. Earlier spam updates in previous years sometimes came with hints — link spam, scaled content abuse. This one came with nothing but the dashboard entry. That silence is deliberate. When Google names the target, spammers optimise around the announcement; when it stays quiet, the detection systems do the talking. The practical reading: assume the enforcement got better at catching the obvious stuff, and don't wait for Google to tell you what "the obvious stuff" is.

## Spam update vs core update: know which beast you're dealing with

Business owners conflate these constantly, and agencies sometimes encourage the confusion because "core update damage" sounds billable. They are different things with different implications.

| | Spam update | Core update |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it changes | Automated systems that detect spam-policy violations | How Google assesses relevance and quality overall |
| Typical frequency | A few times a year (four in 2026) | A few times a year |
| Target | Sites violating published spam policies | Broad reassessment; no specific "target" |
| Manual action involved? | No — algorithmic | No — algorithmic |
| If you were hit | Something on your site likely violates a spam policy | Your content may simply be reassessed as less helpful relative to competitors |
| Recovery path | Remove the violating pattern, wait for re-evaluation | Improve content quality broadly; wait for the next core update |
| Latest confirmed | September 2026 (rolling out now) | May 2026 |

The distinction decides your response. A spam-update hit means there is something specific to find and remove. A core-update drop means the competitive bar moved and your content needs to be genuinely better, not just "fixed." Misdiagnosing one as the other is how businesses spend six months on the wrong project.

## How to tell if the update actually hit you

Most businesses that email us during an update week were not hit by the update. They hit a seasonal dip, a technical bug, or — our favourite — they checked rankings on a phone in a different city and panicked. Before you do anything, run this diagnostic properly. It takes about thirty minutes.

![A magnifying lens hovering over glowing analytics bars and charts, with a few fading red data points dropping away](/blog/google-september-2026-spam-update/fig1.webp)

**Step 1: Fix the date range.** In Search Console, compare the period from 24 September to today against the previous 28 days. Not year-on-year, not "it feels lower than August" — the four weeks immediately before the rollout. If the rollout is still marked active on the dashboard, remember you are looking at a partial picture.

**Step 2: Look at pages, not the site average.** A genuine spam-update hit shows a sharp drop on specific pages or page types — usually the ones with the thinnest content or the most aggressive monetisation. If every page dropped evenly by 8%, that smells like a site-wide technical issue or seasonality, not a spam classifier. Segment by page in the Performance report and sort by largest absolute click loss.

**Step 3: Segment by country and query.** A spam classifier tends to hit the pages, not the geography. If the drop is concentrated in one country, check for regional causes first — currency shifts, local holidays, a competitor's campaign. Pakistan's e-commerce sites, for example, often misread the pre-Eid or sale-season traffic waves as algorithm events.

**Step 4: Rule out the boring causes.** Check these before blaming Google: accidental noindex tags from a deploy, a robots.txt change, expired SSL, server downtime, a broken canonical tag, or — the one we see most in September 2026 — pages dropped from Google's index because of crawl budget issues after a site migration. Also confirm your Shopping feed integration if you run e-commerce: Google retired its old Content API for Shopping on 18 August 2026 in favour of the Merchant API, and from 1 September stores on the old system can see product listings fail intermittently. That looks exactly like a traffic collapse in Search Console and has nothing to do with any update.

**Step 5: Wait out the window.** Even with a clear drop, do not start ripping things out while the rollout is active. Rankings can and do bounce during a two-week rollout as systems settle. Our studio's policy: diagnose now, document everything, act after the dashboard marks the update complete.

One more discipline worth building: annotate. Drop a marker in your analytics for 24 September 2026 with "Google Sept spam update — rollout to ~8 Oct." Six months from now, when someone asks why Q4 organic traffic dipped, that annotation is worth more than any report.

## The spam policies, translated into plain business English

Google's spam policies are written for webmasters. Here is what they mean for a business owner, stripped of jargon. If any of these describe your site, this update week is your warning shot — not because Google announced a crackdown on your industry, but because the detection systems just got sharper.

**Scaled content with no original value.** This is the big one in 2026. AI made it cheap to publish hundreds of pages; Google's systems are trained to spot content that says nothing a human expert wouldn't. The tell: pages that could have been written about any company in any city, with the business name swapped in. A Lahore plumbing company does not need 200 suburb pages that all say the same thing with different place names. Neither does a SaaS blog need 40 listicles summarising other people's listicles.

**Thin affiliate and comparison pages.** Google draws a line between affiliate content that adds original testing, data, or judgement and pages that rephrase the merchant's product description with a buy button. If your "best X" page has never touched the product, the policy considers it thin. This one bites Pakistani and US affiliate publishers equally — and note that affiliate publishers relying on commercial-intent pages should watch this rollout especially closely, since a sustained ranking change on a small cluster of money pages can move revenue even when overall traffic looks steady.

**Doorway pages.** Multiple pages built to rank for slight keyword variations, all funnelling to the same place. The classic is the "service + city" template farm. A handful of genuinely localised location pages is fine; 300 near-duplicates is a doorway network.

**Link schemes.** Bought links, excessive link exchanges, and private blog networks. The Pakistani SEO market still has vendors selling "500 backlinks for PKR 15,000" — we wrote about [what cheap SEO actually buys you](/blog/seo-services-cost-pakistan-2026), and this is the punchline. Those links are exactly the patterns SpamBrain is trained to recognise. If a past vendor built links you can't account for, this update is a good moment to audit them.

**Cloaking, hacked content, and expired-domain abuse.** Less common for legitimate businesses, but check: compromised sites often serve spam to Googlebot while showing owners a clean page. If your traffic dropped and your content looks fine, scan for hacks before assuming a content problem.

**Site reputation abuse.** Publishing third-party content on a trusted domain to borrow its rankings — the "parasite SEO" model. Google has been explicit about this one, and enforcement keeps tightening.

Be honest with yourself here. Most business owners we talk to genuinely don't know what's in their backlink profile or how their blog content was produced. A proper spam-policy audit — crawling the site, reviewing the link profile, sampling content quality — is a few days of work. At our studio, a technical SEO audit with a spam-policy review runs in the PKR 60,000–150,000 range (roughly $200–$550), depending on site size. That is cheap insurance against finding out about a violation from a traffic graph.

## If your traffic dropped: the honest recovery playbook

Suppose you ran the diagnostic and the drop is real, page-level, and timed to the rollout. Here is what recovery actually involves — no sugar-coating, because the SEO industry sells a lot of it.

![Fragmented glowing web-page pieces being reassembled by beams of light over a dark background](/blog/google-september-2026-spam-update/fig2.webp)

**First: find the violating pattern, specifically.** "Our traffic dropped" is not a diagnosis. Which pages? What do they have in common — thin content, bought links pointing at them, aggressive affiliate layouts? The pattern is the diagnosis. Until you can name it in one sentence, you are guessing, and guessing during a penalty recovery is how sites stay penalised.

**Second: remove or fix the pattern completely.** Half-measures don't work with algorithmic classifiers. If it's scaled thin content, the pages need genuine improvement or removal — not a paragraph added to each. If it's bad links, disavow or remove them, but do it surgically: blanket-disavowing your whole profile can hurt. If it's doorway pages, consolidate them into genuinely useful pages with 301 redirects.

**Third: understand there is no reconsideration request.** This trips people up. Manual penalties get reconsideration requests; algorithmic updates don't. Nobody at Google is going to review your cleanup and flip a switch. Your site gets re-evaluated when the systems refresh, which happens on Google's schedule, not yours.

**Fourth: budget months, not days.** [Egochi's response guide](https://www.egochi.com/blog/google-september-2026-spam-update/) states it bluntly: recovery means removing the violating pattern and waiting months. In our studio's observed range, legitimate recoveries take 3–9 months, and some sites never fully return to previous levels because competitors filled the gap while they were down. A full cleanup engagement — link audit and remediation, content overhaul, technical fixes — typically runs $2,000–$6,000 for a small-to-mid business site, more for large ones. Anyone promising recovery in 30 days is selling you the same shortcuts that caused the problem.

**Fifth: document the recovery in Search Console terms.** After the fix, watch for the pattern to reverse at the page level: impressions recovering first, then clicks, usually starting with brand and long-tail queries. Recovery rarely arrives as a single jump; it creeps back over weeks. That creep is normal. Don't "re-fix" a recovering site.

And the uncomfortable truth worth stating: some sites hit by spam updates were built on a model that no longer works. If your business depended on 500 auto-generated pages ranking for long-tail queries, the fix isn't a tactic — it's a strategy change. The economics of spam content broke the day AI made it free for everyone, because Google's defences scale with the threat.

## Frequently asked questions

**When did the September 2026 spam update start?**

**Is the September 2026 spam update a penalty?**
No. It's an algorithmic improvement to Google's spam-detection systems, not a manual penalty, so there's no reconsideration request to file. Sites following Google's spam policies usually see no change, and some gain traffic as spammy competitors drop out of the results.

**How do I know if my site was hit?**
In Search Console, compare clicks and impressions since 24 September against the previous four weeks, then drill into which pages and countries moved. A genuine hit shows a sharp, page-level drop timed to the rollout. Rule out technical causes first, and wait until the two-week window finishes before concluding anything.

**How long does recovery from a spam update take?**
Months, not days. Remove the violating pattern completely — thin scaled content, bought links, doorway pages — then wait for Google's systems to re-evaluate the site. In our studio's observed range, legitimate recoveries take 3–9 months. There is no shortcut and no one at Google to appeal to.

**Should I change my SEO strategy because of this update?**
Probably not much. The update enforces existing policies, so clean sites should monitor rather than overhaul. **What should I absolutely not do during the rollout?**
Don't redesign the site, don't bulk-delete pages, don't upload a panicked disavow file, and don't buy a recovery package from a cold email. Reacting before the rollout finishes is the most common way businesses turn a small dip into lasting damage.
